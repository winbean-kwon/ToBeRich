# Diagnosing Overfitting in Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning for Portfolio Allocation: A Failure-Mode Cascade and Consistency-First Robustness Methodology Across Three Markets

*Target venue: KDD Applied Data Science track / ACM ICAIF (2027 cycle). Draft status: Abstract + Introduction, first pass.*

---

## Abstract

Hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL) methods such as EarnHFT have shown strong results for single-asset, discrete-position high-frequency trading, but their behavior when ported to continuous, multi-asset portfolio allocation is largely undocumented. This paper is an empirical case study and diagnostic methodology rather than a new algorithm: we catalog the failure modes that emerge when porting EarnHFT-style hierarchical RL to portfolio allocation, and report validation-period and seed-level evidence alongside headline test-period metrics. We report an extended case study — spanning a Korean equity market (KOSPI, 781 stocks) and a cryptocurrency market (44 USDT pairs on Binance) — in which a naive single-policy PPO agent falls far behind an equal-weight benchmark on crypto (−75.84% vs. −0.81%); together with a same-direction failure first observed on KOSPI (+0.28% vs. +34.33%), this motivated a hierarchical redesign. The KOSPI failure did not survive a later rerun that fixed defects and leakage in the embedding pipeline: the corrected single-policy PPO beat equal-weight in 9 of 10 runs, but its edge is almost fully reproduced by a static portfolio holding the training-period average cluster weights (Section 7). All backtest figures use an accounting in which turnover is measured against post-return (drifted) holdings and untradable assets (pre-listing or post-delisting) are excluded (Section 3.3). Directly porting EarnHFT's three-stage architecture without its Q-teacher pretraining stage produces a set of agents trained under distinct risk preferences (a "specialist pool") whose real, positive pre-cost signal (+11.98%) is entirely erased by 30-minute-cadence rebalancing costs (net −25.53%); an inference-time inertia filter recovers this signal (+11.14% net) with no retraining, but the seemingly more principled fix of baking the same inertia into training instead badly damages the underlying alpha (pre-cost alpha falls from +11.98% to +2.31%). We conjecture this reflects weakened credit assignment — a looser correspondence between the policy's actions and the rewards it receives — but did not test this directly. A more faithful reimplementation of EarnHFT's selection procedure — still without a Q-teacher — surfaces a second, independent failure: the specialist pool collapses to near-uniform weights (no diversity engine). Restoring diversity by structurally sparsifying the action space (top-K selection with a risk gate, adapting FreQuant and DeepClair) produces measurably different specialists, but the router placed on top of it is later shown, via a decisive ablation, to have collapsed into a constant function equivalent to a single specialist agent — meaning the entire routing stage contributed zero value in this setting. Comparing all specialists across a held-out validation period and the test period shows that the router-favored specialist's apparent test-set superiority (test Sharpe 3.033) does not replicate in validation (validation Sharpe 0.189). We select the final specialist by validation Sharpe alone (the $\beta=-30$ specialist with inference-time exponential smoothing, no router) and report the validation/test Sharpe gap as a supplementary diagnostic. We compare this deployed policy against 16 independent random seeds, a Deflated Sharpe Ratio correction, a leakage-free clean-validation re-run, an ensemble baseline, a transaction-cost sweep, and both classical (buy-and-hold, cross-sectional momentum, mean-variance optimization, risk parity) and deep-learning (EIIE, LSRE-CAAN) portfolio baselines. The deployed policy is the only one of the ten strategies in Table 2 with positive Sharpe in both validation and test (0.914 and 1.310); classical momentum earns roughly 15x the raw test return but loses money in validation, giving a validation/test Sharpe gap of 2.490. However, when the same configuration is trained with other seeds, 13 of 16 have negative validation Sharpe, and the clean retrain also loses money in validation — positive performance in both periods is a property of the deployed seed rather than of the configuration. Applying the same methodology to a multi-year walk-forward on a 13-asset point-in-time universe also fails, in every split, to achieve a small validation/test gap together with positive performance (Section 7). We argue that the resulting failure-mode catalog, together with diagnostics showing why validation-period performance and seed-level distributions must be reported alongside headline test metrics, has standalone value for practitioners porting single-asset HRL trading methods to portfolio settings.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation: single-policy RL with embedding-based state repeatedly fails at portfolio allocation

Reinforcement learning has been proposed repeatedly as a way to learn portfolio allocation policies directly from data, sidestepping the need for hand-specified return and risk models. In practice, however, a plain single-policy agent trained end-to-end on a portfolio's asset universe is a fragile baseline. We first observed this failure in two structurally unrelated markets. On a Korean equity universe of 781 stocks, a PPO agent operating over a 325-dimensional state (five cluster embeddings plus current weights) and a 5-dimensional cluster-weight action space returned +0.28% over a 2024–2025 test window, against an equal-weight benchmark of +34.33% (Sharpe 0.100 vs. 1.043). On a cryptocurrency universe of 44 USDT spot pairs on Binance, the same architectural pattern — a single PPO policy over asset-level embeddings and softmax portfolio weights — returned −75.84% over a 2026 H1 test window (Sharpe −4.134), against an equal-weight benchmark of −0.81% (Sharpe 0.255). That two experiments differing in asset class, state design, action dimensionality, and training period failed in the same direction is what motivated a hierarchical redesign rather than further tuning of the single-policy baseline. A later audit, however, traced the Korean result to pipeline defects. The embedding extraction skipped RevIN normalization and fed features in a different order than in training, the DTW clustering window and the backbone training window overlapped the test period, and with identical code and seed, swapping only the backbone flipped the result from +0.28% to +137.22%. With these defects fixed and the single-policy PPO retrained on two backbones × five seeds, it beat equal-weight's test Sharpe in 9 of 10 runs (Chronos+iTransformer backbone +44.2%±9.3% / Sharpe 1.09±0.22; Chronos+MTGNN backbone +41.7%±2.6% / 1.07±0.09; equal-weight +31.0% / 0.943; Section 7). That edge, however, is hard to read as market timing: a static portfolio holding the training-period average cluster weights throughout the test period performs almost identically (Sharpe 1.108 / 1.099 vs. PPO 1.134 / 1.168 at seed 42). The motivation this paper starts from therefore narrows to the crypto observation and the U.S. large-cap controlled experiment below.

Both observations share a pretrained, embedding-based state representation and a dense softmax action space over the full asset universe. A third, independent check reported in Section 7 — 44 large-cap U.S. equities, first using a raw price-window state with no embeddings — did not reproduce this failure: single-policy PPO finished the 2024–2025 test window essentially tied with the equal-weight benchmark (+45.58%/Sharpe 1.482 vs. +45.00%/Sharpe 1.454). Because this check varies the state representation along with the market, it could not be read as isolating the market variable alone — so we ran a follow-up on the same U.S. equity market and the same test window, this time with Chronos zero-shot embeddings (the same family of state representation used for Korean equities and crypto), holding the market fixed and controlling for architecture (Section 7). Result: single-policy performance moved from "tied" to "clearly behind" equal-weight (test Sharpe 1.482 → 1.251, against equal-weight's 1.458), accompanied by a roughly 8x rise in average turnover (1.22% to 9.2–9.5% per step) — the same cost-driven mechanism documented for v1 below ("turnover erodes alpha"). That is, **architecture (an embedding-based dense action space) is a genuine causal factor** in the failure, though not as catastrophic here as on crypto (Sharpe −4.134) — market or regime appears to modulate the severity of the failure. This paper takes the failure observed, consistent in direction though varying in magnitude, in the two markets where an embedding-based architecture was tested cleanly (crypto, U.S. equities; Section 7) as the motivation for a hierarchical redesign; the corrected Korean rerun did not reproduce it.

### 1.2 A failure-mode cascade when porting EarnHFT to continuous portfolios

Rather than leave the cause of single-policy failure as an ungrounded hypothesis, we find a direct precedent in EarnHFT's own reported results. EarnHFT compares against exactly this kind of single-policy baseline (PPO, DQN) in its own (single-asset, high-frequency) setting, and these baselines underperform EarnHFT's hierarchical architecture across all four of its crypto datasets (BTCUSDT, BTCTUSDT, ETHUSDT, GALAUSDT) — EarnHFT leads on total return in all four and on Sharpe ratio in three of four, with PPO and DQN frequently producing negative returns (e.g., on ETHUSDT: PPO −22.61%, DQN −11.02%, vs. EarnHFT +4.52%; their Table 2). EarnHFT's authors attribute this to single policy-based agents converging to a "dummy policy" and single value-based agents lacking robustness across market-trend shifts (Qin et al., 2024) — precisely the kind of failure we independently observe in Section 1.1 (a single PPO policy trailing equal-weight by a wide margin on crypto — initially also seen on KOSPI, though that failure did not reproduce once the pipeline defects were fixed). This precedent — not an abstract expectation from hierarchical RL theory in general, but EarnHFT's own demonstration that this exact failure pattern is addressed by hierarchical decomposition in its own setting — is why this paper selects EarnHFT's three-stage structure as the architecture to port.

EarnHFT (Qin et al., 2024) addresses a related but narrower problem — high-frequency, single-asset, discrete-position trading — with a three-stage hierarchy: a dynamic-programming "Q-teacher" (Stage I) that pretrains a large set of agents biased toward distinct risk preferences, a distillation stage that retains a small diverse subset (Stage II), and a router that switches between them based on market regime (Stage III). We report what happens when this architecture is ported to a setting EarnHFT was not designed for — continuous portfolio weights over 44 simultaneously-held assets — and, critically, without Stage I, which presupposes a discrete, single-asset action space and is not straightforward to generalize.

Below, we call the set of agents individually trained for a given risk-preference value $\beta$ — not one policy, but a bundle of several agents with distinct dispositions — the **specialist pool**. Stage III's role is to have the router pick, from this specialist pool, whichever member fits the current market conditions.

The resulting investigation surfaces a cascade of failures, each with its own diagnosis and attempted fix, which forms the empirical core of this paper:

- **Direct port (v1).** Reproducing Stages II–III without a Q-teacher (using only priority-weighted sampling in place of Stage I) yields a specialist pool and router that lose −25.53% net of trading costs. Decomposing the result shows the underlying signal is positive (+11.98% *before* costs, already beating the benchmark) but is erased by transaction costs from 30-minute rebalancing at 4.38% average per-step turnover.
- **Inference-time fix works; training-time fix badly damages the signal.** Applying an exponential inertia filter at inference time only (`w_new = (1-α)w_old + α·softmax(a)`, α=0.01) recovers +11.14% net of costs (Sharpe 0.637) without any retraining. Attempting the more principled-seeming alternative — baking the same inertia into the training environment so the policy learns low-turnover behavior directly — instead cuts the pre-cost alpha from +11.98% to +2.31% and nets −2.11%, below the +5.42% obtained by applying the same α=0.03 inertia at inference time only. We conjecture that this reflects weakened correspondence between the actions the policy takes and the rewards it receives — what RL calls **credit assignment**: when the environment itself damps the executed action, the policy may struggle to learn which actions actually produced good outcomes. We did not measure this directly (Section 4.2).
- **A faithful reimplementation still lacks a diversity engine (v3).** Reimplementing EarnHFT more closely — adding a cash action and a strict train/validation split for agent selection, as in the original paper — ends up essentially level with equal weight (−0.02% vs. −0.81% net; Sharpe 0.284 vs. 0.255), because the pool of specialist agents converges to near-identical, near-uniform policies. We connect this to the absence of Stage I: in our implementation and training budget, using the risk-preference parameter only to bias data sampling, without a Q-teacher injecting distinct behavioral targets into the loss, did not produce diversity in a continuous, 45-dimensional softmax action space.
- **Restoring diversity via a structural action-space change (v4).** Replacing the dense softmax action with a structurally sparse one — top-K asset selection (adapting FreQuant, Jeon et al., KDD'24) combined with a dedicated risk-exposure gate (adapting DeepClair, Choi et al., CIKM'24) — produces measurable behavioral differences across the specialist pool (turnover does not decay to zero over the test window; top-10 holdings rotate through 33 of 44 assets) and, combined with inference-time smoothing and **with the router still in place**, yields +9.37% net of costs, Sharpe 2.314, MDD −5.01% — a number the next two bullets reinterpret once the router itself is shown to be doing nothing.
- **The router collapses to a constant function.** A subsequent fix targeting a train/serve mismatch in the router's inputs appears, at first, to further improve risk-adjusted results (Sharpe 2.314 → 3.033). A decisive follow-up ablation shows this improvement is illusory: running the specialist the router selects ($\beta=30$) alone, with no router at all, reproduces the "improved" result exactly (+8.03%, Sharpe 3.033). The router had not learned to route — it had degenerated into always selecting one fixed agent.
- **That one agent's test performance does not replicate in validation.** Re-evaluating all four candidate specialist agents on both a held-out validation period and the test period shows that the router-favored $\beta=30$ swings from validation Sharpe 0.189 to test Sharpe 3.033. Selecting by validation Sharpe alone picks $\beta=-30$ (validation 0.914, test 1.310), the only specialist of the four positive in both periods. We argue that validation-period performance and the validation/test Sharpe gap should be reported alongside headline test-period numbers.

### 1.3 Validating the survivor

The final deployed policy — a single specialist agent with inference-time exponential smoothing and no router — is deliberately the simplest configuration to survive this cascade. Rather than treat this simplicity as a stopping point, we subject it to a battery of robustness checks aimed at the kind of failures uncovered above: reproducibility across 16 independent training seeds (in test, 14 of 16 have positive Sharpe and 11 beat equal weight, but only 3 — including the deployed seed — have positive validation Sharpe, so the deployed seed's two-period consistency is not typical of the configuration); a Deflated Sharpe Ratio correction for the multiple-seed selection process (which, counterintuitively, does *not* penalize the seed with the highest raw Sharpe more than others); a leakage-free retraining using a held-out validation window never seen during training (test performance close to the deployed model's, +6.39% vs. +6.66%, but a loss in validation); a weight-averaging ensemble of seeds (higher test Sharpe but negative validation Sharpe, widening the validation/test gap); a transaction-cost sensitivity sweep from 0.5x to 2x the deployed rate (test Sharpe at least 4x equal weight's throughout, Section 6.9); and a comparison against four classical baselines (buy-and-hold, cross-sectional momentum, Ledoit-Wolf minimum-variance optimization, risk parity) and two deep-learning baselines adapted from the literature (EIIE, Jiang et al., 2017; LSRE-CAAN, Li, Zhang, Yang, & Chen, 2023). The top-K width sweep (Section 6.8) was run under the pre-correction environment and is not part of this recomputation. Under a raw-return criterion, momentum wins by a wide margin (+97.41% vs. +6.66% test return). But momentum loses money in validation (−18.21%), giving a validation/test Sharpe gap of 2.490, second only to the $\beta=30$ specialist (2.844); the deployed policy's gap (0.396) is the smallest in Table 2. The EIIE and LSRE-CAAN baselines show the same near-uniform convergence first observed in v3 across two entirely different network architectures (within 0.04 Sharpe of their own notebook's equal weight).

### 1.4 Contributions

This paper makes the following contributions:

1. **A causal isolation of why single-policy RL fails.** After observing that embedding-based single-policy RL trails equal-weight by a wide margin on cryptocurrency (an initially similar Korean-equity failure did not survive a rerun with pipeline defects fixed — Section 7), we run a confound-controlling experiment on a third market (U.S. large-cap equities) that holds the market fixed and varies only the state representation (raw price windows vs. pretrained embeddings), directly confirming that this failure is caused by the embedding-based dense action space itself (accompanied by a roughly eightfold rise in turnover) rather than by market-specific chance — with market or regime independently modulating how severe the resulting collapse is (Section 7).
2. **A documented failure-mode cascade** for porting single-asset, discrete-position hierarchical RL (EarnHFT) to continuous, multi-asset portfolios: cost-driven signal erasure, a training-time inertia trap, a Q-teacher-shaped diversity gap, a structural fix via sparse action spaces, and a router that silently degenerates into a constant function. The structure itself — specialist pool + router + overall-exposure control — already exists in prior work such as MoEDRLPM and DeepTrader (Sections 2.1–2.2); the contribution here is the record of this cascade, not the structure.
3. **A robustness-diagnostic methodology** — reporting the validation/test Sharpe gap together with the seed-level distribution, shown to be complementary to (not redundant with) the Deflated Sharpe Ratio — exercised across 16 seeds, an ensemble, a leakage-free holdout, and classical and deep-learning baselines. The diagnostic exposed that the router-favored specialist ($\beta=30$) and momentum do not replicate their test advantage in validation, but it also exposed that the deployed seed's consistency does not replicate across other seeds of the same configuration (Section 6.1). Two supplementary formal significance tests (DSR, a block permutation test) do not reach conventional significance.
4. **A simple deployed policy and an honest account of its limits** (one specialist agent, no router, inference-time smoothing) — the only one of the ten strategies in Table 2 with positive Sharpe in both validation and test, cutting maximum drawdown to −7.35% against the equal-weight benchmark's −40.46% (an 82% reduction) — though this reduction comes mostly from low average exposure (about 18%): holding equal weight along the same exposure path gives a test MDD of −8.28% and Sharpe 1.207 (Section 6.11). However, most other seeds of the same configuration and the clean retrain lose money in validation (Sections 6.1, 6.3), and the point-in-time multi-year walk-forward in Section 7 fails to achieve consistency in all five (validation, test) observations (Section 8, Limitation 5). The policy's two-period consistency should therefore be read as specific to the deployed seed, and its downside-protection profile should not be read as evidence of capital preservation validated over a long horizon. This policy is currently running as a live trading system on a real exchange account (see Section 8, Limitation 10, for caveats on the live record's current length), but that is a report of continued operation, not additional evidence of robustness.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Hierarchical reinforcement learning for trading

EarnHFT (Qin et al., 2024, AAAI'24; arXiv:2309.12891) is the architectural template this paper builds on and departs from. It targets second-level, single-asset, discrete-position high-frequency trading with a three-stage pipeline: **Stage I** solves a dynamic program offline to obtain an approximately optimal action-value function $Q^*$ for a large number of risk-preference settings $\beta$, and trains a pool of agents via DDQN with a KL-divergence term $\mathrm{KL}(Q_\theta \| Q^*)$ against this teacher signal plus imitation of the optimal actor (their Eq. 2–3), using $\beta$-conditioned priority sampling of training chunks (their Eq. 4); **Stage II** distills this large pool down to a small number of representative agents by evaluating each on a held-out validation split, per market regime; **Stage III** trains a router (in the original paper, a DDQN over market/agent features) that switches between the surviving specialists as the regime changes. The dynamic-programming teacher in Stage I is what allows the pool to be diverse by construction — each agent is trained toward a different, externally supplied optimal policy, rather than discovering diversity on its own.

Hierarchical RL for multi-asset portfolio allocation is, on its own, not unstudied, and this paper does not claim otherwise. HRPM (Wang, Wei, An, Feng, & Yao, 2021, AAAI'21) splits portfolio management into a high-level policy that sets multi-asset weights at low frequency and a low-level policy that executes each rebalance as a sequence of bid/ask orders to minimize slippage — a strategic-allocation/execution split, not the risk-preference-conditioned specialist pool used here. Zha, Dai, Xu, & Wu (2022, IJCNN'22; DOI 10.1109/IJCNN55064.2022.9892378) split the problem the other way: a high-level policy selects a subset of stocks with high profit probability from a large universe, and a low-level policy sets continuous weights only over that selected subset — a selection-then-allocation hierarchy, again without a risk-preference-conditioned pool or a regime router. MetaTrader (Niu, Li, & Li, 2022, CIKM'22; arXiv:2210.01774) pretrains multiple diverse trading policies via a combined RL/imitation objective and then learns a meta-policy that routes among them — structurally the closest of these to EarnHFT's Stage II–III, but pool diversity here is induced by the imitation objective rather than a DP teacher, and the setting is multi-asset from the start rather than ported from a single-asset origin. Millea & Edalat (2023, *International Journal of Financial Studies*, 11(1), 10; DOI 10.3390/ijfs11010010) route, via a DRL agent framed as a stateful multi-armed bandit, among several off-market Hierarchical Risk Parity / Hierarchical Equal Risk Contribution allocators, sidestepping the difficulty of learning continuous weights directly by keeping the low-level allocators non-learned. MacroHFT (Zong, Wang, Qin, Feng, Wang, & An, 2024, KDD'24; arXiv:2406.14537) — a follow-up from an overlapping author set with EarnHFT — extends EarnHFT's own single-asset, high-frequency setting with regime-decomposed sub-agents and a memory-augmented hyper-agent that mixes their outputs, but remains single-asset throughout. Most recently, HARLF (Coriat & Benhamou, 2025; arXiv:2507.18560, a non-peer-reviewed preprint at the time of writing) proposes a three-tier base-agent/meta-agent/super-agent hierarchy for multi-asset allocation augmented with LLM-derived sentiment. Closest of all to our v4 structure is **MoEDRLPM** (Wei, Chen, Zhang, Wen, Nie, & Xie, 2025, *Applied Intelligence*, 55(5), 347; DOI 10.1007/s10489-025-06242-6): it builds stock representations with a spatio-temporal adaptive embedding and temporal/spatial self-attention, uses a router to dynamically select the current best expert from a mixture-of-experts pool and aggregates the experts' decisions into portfolio weights, separately models current market risk from index data to set the invested-capital ratio, and optimizes the whole with DRL (SSE50, CSI300). The combination "specialist pool + router + overall-exposure control for a multi-asset portfolio" therefore already exists, and this paper does not claim to propose it. As far as we can tell from its abstract (we did not compare against the full text), MoEDRLPM does not report an ablation testing whether its router is non-constant, nor a validation/test consistency diagnostic; in that sense our Sections 4.5–4.6 — where a router of the same shape degenerates into the single-expert policy — can be read as a cautionary case report for that design. None of these seven papers, however, attempts to port EarnHFT's specific three-stage pipeline — a DP-teacher-trained specialist pool plus validation-based distillation plus a regime router — onto a continuous multi-asset simplex, and consequently none reports the specific failure sequence this paper documents: signal erosion under realistic transaction costs, diversity collapse in the specialist pool once the DP teacher is removed, and a regime router degenerating into a constant function. That is the gap Section 4 fills, and it is the basis for this paper's narrower claim of contribution (Section 1.4) rather than any claim that hierarchical RL for multi-asset portfolios is itself novel.

This construction is specific to a single, liquid asset with a discretized action space (long / flat / short, in the typical instantiation), where an exact or near-exact DP solution is tractable. It does not directly generalize to a continuous-weight, multi-asset portfolio, where the action space is a $(N{-}1)$-simplex over $N$ assets and no equivalent closed-form optimum is available. Section 4 documents, in detail, what happens when Stages II–III are ported to such a setting while Stage I is replaced with a cheaper substitute (risk-preference-conditioned data sampling only, no teacher signal) — to our knowledge this specific failure mode (diversity collapse without a teacher, and the router's subsequent collapse into a constant function) has not been previously documented for portfolio-scale HRL.

More generally, EarnHFT's three-stage structure is one concrete instance of the options framework (Sutton, Precup, & Singh, 1999) — a fixed set of sub-policies with a higher-level policy switching among them — and belongs to the same "long-horizon manager, short-horizon executor" family as feudal RL (Dayan & Hinton, 1993; extended to deep learning by Vezhnevets et al.'s (2017) FeUdal Networks). These general theories tend to presuppose that sub-policy diversity arises naturally from distinct sub-goals or termination conditions; this paper's failure catalog (Sections 4.3, 4.5) provides a concrete instance of exactly that general form breaking down when the presupposition (played by the Q-teacher in EarnHFT) is absent.

### 2.2 Structured action spaces and policy regularization for portfolio selection

Two recent papers supply the structural ingredients we use to restore specialist diversity once the direct EarnHFT port fails. **FreQuant** (Jeon, Park, Park, & Kang, 2024, KDD'24; DOI 10.1145/3637528.3671668) proposes a frequency-domain RL framework for portfolio optimization built around a Frequency State Encoder that fuses abrupt market events with prevalent multi-frequency patterns; the component we adopt is its confidence-score-based top-$K$ asset selection mechanism (their Eq. 13–14), which we use as a way to sparsify the action space rather than to encode frequency information — the frequency-domain encoder itself is not part of this paper's contribution and is not reimplemented. **DeepClair** (Choi, Kim, Gim, Lee, & Kang, 2024, CIKM'24; arXiv:2407.13427) pretrains a Transformer-based market forecaster and fine-tunes it with LoRA into an RL-based portfolio-selection pipeline; the component we adopt is its dedicated risk-exposure gate that separates *which assets to hold* from *how much total risk to take*, which we implement as a single scalar sigmoid gate $\rho \in (0,1)$ applied on top of the top-$K$ selection. As with FreQuant, DeepClair's forecasting-pretraining stage is not reimplemented — in our pipeline, that role is already filled by the Chronos+MTGNN embedding backbone described in Section 3.2. This separation of *which assets* from *how much total exposure* predates DeepClair: **DeepTrader** (Wang, Huang, Tu, Zhang, & Xu, 2021, AAAI'21; DOI 10.1609/aaai.v35i1.16144) combines an asset scoring unit (rewarded by price rising rate, with inter-asset relations captured by an estimated causal-structure graph) with a market scoring unit that embeds macro market conditions and dynamically adjusts the long/short fund ratio (rewarded by negative maximum drawdown). Our top-$K$ selection plus risk gate $\rho$ is functionally in the same family as this two-unit split (differing in that we adjust only the long/cash ratio, with no short side), and the finding of Section 6.11 — that the deployed policy's low maximum drawdown comes mostly from the low average exposure produced by this gate — points in the same direction as the effect DeepTrader's market unit targets. Both adoptions are therefore partial: we borrow a single structural mechanism from each paper and graft it onto the EarnHFT skeleton, rather than reproducing either paper in full. Section 4.4 describes the resulting environment (`SparseGatedCryptoPortfolioEnv`) precisely. For the same reason, we do not include full reimplementations of FreQuant or DeepClair as baselines in Section 6: a fair comparison would require matching each paper's own frequency-decomposition or forecasting-pretraining budget rather than substituting ours, and doing so for two additional full architectures is a substantially larger undertaking than the topology-only EIIE and LSRE-CAAN baselines we do include (Section 6.6, Limitation 8) — left for future work.

The inertia filter of Section 4.2 relates to a separate literature — action policy smoothing — but as a contrast rather than an adoption. **CAPS** (Mysore, Mabsout, Mancuso, & Saenko, 2021, ICRA'21; arXiv:2012.06644) smooths RL-controller actions for robotic control (e.g., quadrotors) by adding two regularization terms directly to the policy loss: a temporal-smoothness term requiring consecutive actions to resemble one another, and a spatial-smoothness term requiring similar states to map to similar actions, reporting an ~80% reduction in power consumption on a real quadrotor while maintaining flight-worthy control. The second experiment of Section 4.2 (`SmoothStepMixin`, which bakes exponential blending into the training environment itself) is, in fact, an independent rediscovery of the same idea as CAPS's temporal-smoothness term — inducing smoothness directly at training time — applied to this paper's setting. Where CAPS reports success in robotic control, the identical approach here badly damages the cost-free alpha (11.98%→2.31%; we conjecture weakened credit assignment but did not verify it). The solution we ultimately adopt (smoothing applied only at inference time, with full training-time agency at $\alpha=1$) therefore sits at the opposite pole from CAPS: rather than injecting smoothness into the training objective, it leaves training untouched and applies smoothness only as a deployment-time post-process. We conjecture, without verifying, that the key difference between the two settings is reward density — robotic control receives a dense, continuous reward each step, whereas this setting's reward is dominated by a sparse, delayed transaction-cost penalty.

### 2.3 Classical portfolio optimization baselines

We compare against four classical, non-learned strategies as part of the robustness suite in Section 6, alongside the equal-weight benchmark: buy-and-hold, cross-sectional momentum, mean-variance optimization using the Ledoit & Wolf (2004) shrinkage covariance estimator (chosen over sample covariance because the asset universe is small relative to the observation window and highly cross-correlated, a setting sample covariance handles poorly), and risk parity (inverse-volatility weighting). Markowitz (1952) and Black & Litterman (1992) are the canonical references for mean-variance and Bayesian-prior portfolio construction respectively; our MVO baseline follows the unconstrained global-minimum-variance formulation rather than the full mean-variance frontier, since forward-return estimates in this setting are noisy enough that the minimum-variance corner is the more defensible choice. Separately, the top-$K$ sparse selection of Section 4.4 can be read as a differentiable relaxation of cardinality-constrained portfolio optimization from the combinatorial-optimization literature — adding an upper bound on the number of held assets to the mean-variance model (Chang, Meade, Beasley, & Sharaiha, 2000; exact algorithms in Bertsimas & Shioda, 2009) — approximated here by a learned confidence score rather than solved exactly. The Spearman IC diagnostic in Section 6.8, however, shows this learned approximation barely predicts the cross-sectional ranking of future returns, suggesting the "selection based on genuine signal" assumption underlying the classical formulation may not hold for this neural approximation.

### 2.4 Reinforcement-learning algorithms

All specialist and router policies in this paper are trained with off-the-shelf actor-critic and value-based methods rather than a novel RL algorithm: Proximal Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2017) for the continuous-weight specialist pool, and standard Deep Q-Networks (Mnih et al., 2015) for the discrete regime router. The original EarnHFT paper uses DDQN (van Hasselt, Guez, & Silver, 2016) for both Stage I pool training and the Stage III router; this paper substitutes standard DQN for both, for implementation simplicity — a departure from, not a match to, EarnHFT's choice. Section 8 (Limitations) separately discusses what this simplification could contribute to the router-collapse diagnosis in Section 4.5. The contribution of this paper is not to the RL algorithms themselves but to the architecture built on top of them and the diagnostic methodology used to evaluate it.

### 2.5 Backtest-overfitting diagnostics

The closest work to Sections 4.5–4.6 and Section 6 of this paper is Gort, Liu, Sun, Gao, Chen, & Wang (2022; arXiv:2209.05559), who study exactly the failure mode we encounter — a deep RL cryptocurrency trading agent whose backtest performance does not reflect a genuine, reproducible edge — and propose formalizing overfitting detection as a hypothesis test over repeated train/evaluate cycles. Our validation/test Sharpe-gap diagnostic (Section 4.6) began as a simpler, qualitative version of the same idea, and Section 6.10 formalizes it directly as a block permutation test, confirming it quantitatively as well — consistent in direction but short of conventional significance. Section 6 argues this kind of diagnostic should be reported as a statistical test rather than an ad hoc comparison in future work along these lines.

Two tools from the quantitative finance literature give us a way to formalize "how much should a single good backtest be trusted": the **Probability of Backtest Overfitting** (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado, & Zhu, 2016, *Journal of Computational Finance*; SSRN 2326253), which estimates overfitting probability via combinatorially symmetric cross-validation, and the **Deflated Sharpe Ratio** (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014, *Journal of Portfolio Management*), which corrects a Sharpe ratio for the number of independent trials from which it was selected and for non-normality of returns. We apply the latter, rather than PBO, to our 16-seed sweep in Section 6.2: PBO's combinatorially symmetric cross-validation is built to test a single continuous track record by partitioning it into many in-sample/out-of-sample sub-period combinations, whereas our selection problem is choosing the best of $N$ independently-trained candidates (seeds) — a framing DSR's multiple-comparisons correction addresses directly without requiring a sub-period partitioning scheme; adapting PBO's combinatorial logic to a set of independently-trained candidates rather than sub-periods of one series is left for future work. We note there — as an empirical, not merely theoretical, finding — that the Deflated Sharpe Ratio corrects for a *different* form of selection bias than the validation/test consistency check used in Section 4.6: the two diagnostics are complementary rather than substitutable, and a policy can look identical under one while looking very different under the other.

## 3. Problem Setup and Data

### 3.1 Two markets, one design pattern

We study two independently constructed portfolio-allocation problems that share a common design pattern (an embedding backbone feeding a state space, a softmax-family action space over portfolio weights, and a PPO-trained policy) but differ in every empirical detail:

| | KOSPI equities | Cryptocurrency (Binance) |
|---|---|---|
| Universe | 781 KOSPI-listed stocks | 44 USDT spot pairs (of an initially larger set, after excluding 6 symbols with <6 months of history) |
| Bar frequency | Daily | 5-minute |
| Raw features | OHLCV + 20 technical indicators (SMA/EMA/MACD/RSI/Bollinger/ATR family) | Identical 20-indicator set, computed on the 5-minute grid |
| Sequence encoding | RevIN-normalized 60-step patches | Same patching scheme, applied per 5-minute bar |
| Prediction target | 1-day / 5-day forward return | 6-bar (30-minute) forward return, matching the live rebalancing cadence |
| State aggregation | 5 DTW k-means clusters (of 757 clustered stocks); state = per-cluster mean embedding | Per-asset; state = per-asset embedding (no clustering) |
| Action space | 5-dimensional (cluster weights) | Up to 45-dimensional (asset weights [+ cash], see Section 3.3) |
| Training / test window | 2021–2023 / 2024–2025 | 2025 (full year, or a 1–9 month subset, depending on experiment) / 2026 H1 |

The two universes were built independently (different data vendors, different feature pipelines, different clustering vs. no-clustering design decisions) before either track's RL component was designed. We initially took this independence to make the single-policy failure seen in both markets informative, but the KOSPI failure later turned out to be a pipeline artifact (Section 1.1, Section 7). The two tracks should therefore be read not as two instances of one failure but as two applications of one design pattern to different markets. How these design differences bear on the interpretation of the results — and exactly where this paper's generality claims stop — is addressed directly in Section 7.

A further asymmetry matters more than the bar-frequency row of the table above suggests on its own: EarnHFT's original setting (Section 2.1) operates at second-level decision frequency, and both tracks here run several orders of magnitude slower than that — crypto's 30-minute rebalancing cadence already falls well outside what the original paper calls high-frequency, and KOSPI's daily cadence is slower still. This matters most for KOSPI: at a once-a-day rebalancing cadence, the transaction-cost drag that motivates EarnHFT's hierarchical structure in the first place (Section 4.1) has essentially no room to compound the way it does at 30-minute or sub-second intervals. What this means for how the KOSPI results in Section 7 should and should not be read is addressed directly there and in Limitation 1 of Section 8.

### 3.2 Common embedding backbone

Both tracks use the same family of per-asset (or per-cluster) embedding backbone, selected after a systematic comparison of eight candidate time-series backbones on directional accuracy and Spearman rank correlation of return forecasts (StockMixer, PatchTST, iTransformer, Chronos zero-shot, Chronos-LoRA, MTGNN, Mamba/BiGRU, and a Chronos+MTGNN hybrid). In the first comparison (StockMixer, PatchTST, iTransformer, Chronos zero-shot) the zero-shot Chronos encoder (Ansari et al., 2024) was strongest on directional accuracy; in the extended comparison MTGNN (Wu et al., 2020) — an adaptive-graph-learner plus gated temporal convolution originally designed for multivariate forecasting — ranked first overall (directional accuracy 0.5145, Spearman 0.0356). However, every candidate's directional accuracy lies within 0.496–0.515, essentially at the test set's up-move base rate (0.505), and re-running the same configuration moves the metrics by about as much as the gaps between candidates, so this ranking should be read as a weak signal rather than a decisive ordering. The deployed backbone concatenates a frozen pretrained Chronos T5 encoder (a per-asset temporal representation of the closing-price series) with a simplified MTGNN-style graph encoder (the 20 technical indicators of a single asset as nodes, with graph convolution over a learned indicator-to-indicator adjacency matrix and gated dilated temporal convolution), fused by an MLP. The graph models relations among indicators only, not among assets. The hybrid's standalone forecasting performance (directional accuracy 0.5056, Spearman 0.0259) was lower than MTGNN alone. The backbone emits a 64-dimensional embedding per asset (or per cluster, in the KOSPI case) per rebalancing step; this embedding, concatenated with the current portfolio weight vector, is the full state observed by the RL policy in every variant described below. Full backbone comparison numbers and RevIN normalization details (Kim et al., 2022) are reported in the project's supplementary technical log (outside the scope of this paper) and are not repeated here. The embedding backbone is held fixed across all crypto-track RL experiments (Sections 4–6), so the architecture comparisons in Section 4 are not explained by backbone differences. This does not mean, however, that the choice of backbone is irrelevant to RL performance. In the Korean-equity track's original pipeline, identical code and seed gave a single-policy PPO test return of +137.22% with a Chronos+iTransformer backbone and +0.28% with Chronos+MTGNN. That pipeline had defects, including missing embedding normalization, and after they were fixed the two backbones gave similar results (five-seed mean Sharpe 1.09 vs. 1.07, Section 7).

### 3.3 Common environment formalism

Unless otherwise noted, every environment variant in Section 4 shares the following structure. At each step $t$, the agent observes a state $s_t = [\,e_{1,t}, \dots, e_{N,t},\, w_{t-1}\,] \in \mathbb{R}^{N \cdot d + N}$, where $N$ is the number of assets (or clusters), $d = 64$ is the embedding dimension, $e_{i,t}$ is asset $i$'s embedding, and $w_{t-1}$ is the portfolio weight vector carried over from the previous step (for the 44-asset crypto case, $N{\cdot}d + N = 2{,}860$; variants with an explicit cash leg add one further dimension for the previous cash weight). Each embedding $e_{i,t}$ is encoded from data up to and including step $t$ only, keeping the backbone's inputs strictly separated from the forward-looking target return used in the reward below. The policy outputs an action $a_t$, which is mapped to a target weight vector $w_t$ (softmax normalization in the dense variants of Section 4.1–4.3; the top-$K$-plus-gate transform of Section 4.4 in the sparse variant). The environment then charges a proportional transaction cost on the realized turnover,
$$\text{cost}_t = \tau \cdot \lVert w_t - \tilde{w}_{t-1} \rVert_1, \qquad \tilde{w}_{t-1,i} = \frac{w_{t-1,i}\,(1+\rho_{t-1,i})}{1 + w_{t-1}^\top \rho_{t-1}},$$

where $\tilde{w}_{t-1}$ is the actual holding after the previous step's target $w_{t-1}$ has drifted with that step's returns $\rho_{t-1}$ (in variants whose weights sum to less than one, the remainder stays in zero-return cash). The previous weight in the state is also this $\tilde{w}_{t-1}$. A strategy whose target never changes, such as equal weight, therefore still pays the cost of rebalancing back from drifted weights (the same formulation as EIIE, Jiang et al., 2017). Assets with no return data at a step (pre-listing or post-delisting) are treated as untradable and given zero target weight (dense variants renormalize over the rest; the top-$K$ variant selects only among tradable assets and leaves the remainder in cash). An earlier version of this paper carried the previous *target* weights forward as holdings, omitting rebalancing costs and allowing untradable assets to be held as if they were zero-return assets; the crypto performance figures in Sections 4–6 are recomputed with this corrected environment. The exceptions, marked in the text, are the objective-injection experiment in Section 4.3 and the top-$K$ sweep in Section 6.8, which remain pre-correction values. The trained policies themselves were not retrained (Section 8, Limitation 11). $\tau = 0.001$ (10 bps), matching Binance's standard taker fee. This is a linear, fee-only cost model: it does not capture order-book slippage or nonlinear market impact, which can plausibly exceed 10bp at times for a 44-asset book rebalanced every 30 minutes. Section 6.9 tests robustness to a mis-specified linear fee up to 2x the assumed rate, but that sweep does not substitute for a full market-impact model. The environment computes the step reward as the realized portfolio return net of this cost,

$$r_t = w_t^\top \rho_t - \text{cost}_t,$$

where $\rho_t$ is the vector of realized forward asset returns for step $t$ (the pre-computed 6-bar target return described in Section 3.1, so no lookahead is introduced). All policies are trained with PPO (`stable-baselines3`, `MlpPolicy`) using a common hyperparameter budget of learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, $n_\text{steps}=1024$, batch size 64, 10 epochs per update, $\gamma=0.99$, GAE $\lambda=0.95$, clip range 0.2, entropy coefficient 0.01, and value-function coefficient 0.5, over 100k environment steps per specialist agent; the DQN router (Section 4.1, 4.5) uses learning rate $10^{-3}$, replay buffer size 20,000, batch size 64, $\gamma=0.95$, a $[128,64]$ MLP, and $\epsilon$-greedy exploration annealed from 0.3 to 0.05, over a 20k-step budget. These budgets are held fixed across v1–v4 specifically so that the architectural comparisons in Section 4 and Section 5 are not confounded by unequal compute.

## 4. Method: An Architecture Cascade

Each subsection below corresponds to one iteration in the cascade summarized in Section 1.2. We describe the environment or training change first, then the outcome and diagnosis; quantitative results are consolidated in Section 5's ablation tables rather than repeated in full here.

### 4.1 v1 — Direct EarnHFT port without a Q-teacher

Stage A segments the 2025 training-period equal-weight NAV of the 44 assets into regimes by taking local extrema of a low-pass-filtered price series and merging them (via DTW-based similarity) into contiguous chunks labeled with one of five regimes — `bear`, `pullback`, `sideways`, `rally`, `bull` — yielding 123 chunks of roughly 3 days each (rally 44, sideways 29, pullback 24, bear 13, bull 13 — a distribution skewed toward rising regimes). Stage B trains four specialist PPO agents over the dense 44-dimensional softmax action space of Section 3.3, one per risk-preference setting $\beta \in \{-90, -30, 30, 90\}$; in the absence of a DP teacher, $\beta$ is used only to bias the *sampling priority* of training chunks toward regimes where a preference of that sign would historically have been favorable (`chunk_sampling_priority`, with a fixed risk threshold of 0.2), leaving the PPO objective itself unchanged. Stage C trains a DQN router (Section 3.3 hyperparameters) over macro observations to select among the four specialists at each new chunk boundary.

The resulting system loses 25.53% of capital net of trading costs over the test window. Recomputing the same trajectory of actions without the 10 bps transaction cost recovers +11.98% — ahead of the equal-weight benchmark's +2.71% pre-cost (−0.81% net) — and the per-step turnover in the raw system averages 4.38% of portfolio value, compounding to a cumulative cost drag of 33.50% over the window. The signal produced by Stage B+C is therefore real by this measure; it is destroyed entirely by 30-minute-cadence rebalancing costs before it reaches net returns.

### 4.2 Inference-time vs. training-time inertia

The most direct fix for a cost-dominated policy is to reduce turnover. We test two ways of doing this. The first, applied only at inference time with no retraining, blends each step's target weights into the previous weights via a fixed-rate exponential filter,

$$w_t = (1-\alpha)\, w_{t-1} + \alpha \cdot \text{softmax}(a_t),$$

with $\alpha$ swept over $\{1.0, 0.3, 0.15, 0.05, 0.03, 0.02, 0.01\}$; the setting with the best test result, $\alpha=0.01$, brings the same trained policy from −25.53% to +11.14% net of costs (Sharpe 0.637, MDD −37.17%). Because this $\alpha$ was chosen on test-period results, the figure is an exploratory observation, not an independent out-of-sample result (from v3 onward $\alpha$ is chosen on the validation period, and the same value 0.01 is selected; Sections 4.3, 6.3). The cost-free alpha stays positive across the swept range (+7.35% to +12.62%) and is nearly the same at unsmoothed $\alpha=1$ (+11.98%) and at $\alpha=0.01$ (+12.62%) — suggesting the underlying signal operates at a slower frequency than 30 minutes and that inertia mainly shields it from costs. In the same period we also tried eight inference-time overlays on top of the v1 pool, combining a cash gate, a pool-average ensemble, top-10 concentration, and volatility targeting (best: pool-average ensemble with top-10 concentration, +20.41% net, Sharpe 0.942). Because these combinations were chosen by looking at test-period results, they carry selection bias, and we did not carry them forward in the cascade.

The second approach bakes the same exponential blending directly into the training environment (a `SmoothStepMixin` with $\alpha=0.03$), on the reasoning that a policy trained to expect its own actions to be damped might learn to compensate, or might learn an inherently lower-turnover policy from the start (as discussed in Section 2.2, this is an independent rediscovery of the same idea as CAPS's (Mysore, Mabsout, Mancuso, & Saenko, 2021) training-time smoothness regularization for robotic control, applied to this setting). This is clearly worse than inference-time inertia: net return falls to −2.11% (Sharpe 0.216, MDD −40.98%), below the +5.42% of v1 with the same $\alpha=0.03$ inertia applied at inference time only, and — more informatively — the cost-free alpha falls from 11.98% to 2.31%, losing about four-fifths — the opposite of the result CAPS reports for robotic control. We hypothesize this reflects a credit-assignment failure — once the environment itself decouples the executed weight change from the raw action the policy emits, PPO's advantage estimates may no longer point toward actions that helped, degrading the policy's incentive gradient — though we note this mechanism is an interpretation of the outcome, not confirmed by a direct diagnostic such as advantage variance or policy loss, which we did not collect for this run. The practical rule this establishes, and which we carry through the remainder of the paper, is: **train with full agency ($\alpha=1$ during training), and apply inertia only at execution time.**

### 4.3 v3 — A more faithful reimplementation still lacks a diversity engine

To rule out that v1's simplifications (no cash action, priority-sampling instead of a teacher, and test-set-tuned agent selection) were themselves responsible for the shortfall, we build a closer reimplementation of EarnHFT's Stage II/III selection procedure. A 45th action dimension is added representing cash (return 0, no transaction cost on cash-only rebalancing), giving agents with negative $\beta$ a direct way to express de-risking rather than only reallocating among risky assets. The 2025 calendar year is split strictly into a 9-month training period and a 3-month (Q4) validation period (4,416 30-minute rebalancing steps); 20 candidate agents (4 values of $\beta$ $\times$ 5 training checkpoints) are each evaluated per-regime on the validation split, and the best candidate is retained per regime, following the original paper's Stage II procedure. The router and the final $\alpha$ smoothing rate are likewise selected on this validation split rather than on the test period, removing the test-set selection bias present in v1 and its inertia fix.

This version still does not meaningfully beat equal weight: −0.02% net of costs (Sharpe 0.284), essentially level with the equal-weight benchmark (−0.81%, Sharpe 0.255), with the final $\alpha=0.01$ selected on validation. In the original, pre-correction run, inspecting the deployed policy showed the cash weight pinned at 2.2% — exactly $1/45$, the uniform-initialization value — for essentially the entire test window, and the 20 candidate agents' regime-conditional validation scores differ by under one percentage point from each other, i.e., they behave as near-identical clones despite having been trained with four different $\beta$ values. We connect this to the role of EarnHFT's Stage I: in our implementation and training budget, risk preference expressed only through the *sampling distribution* of training data, with the PPO objective itself unchanged, did not produce distinguishable behavior in a 45-dimensional continuous action space — without a teacher signal that injects $\beta$-dependent targets directly into the loss, gradient descent has no reason to move away from a uniform-weight local optimum in either direction.

Rather than leave this interpretation purely post hoc, we test it with a minimal intervention (figures in this paragraph come from the pre-correction environment of Section 3.3 and were not recomputed). Leaving `CashPortfolioEnv` unchanged, we add a single scalar auxiliary term directly to the reward that pushes cash weight in a $\beta$-sign-dependent direction ($\text{aux} = -\lambda \cdot (\beta/90) \cdot (\text{cash} - 0.5)$) — not a reproduction of the full DP-based $Q^*_\beta$ teacher, but a minimal transplant of its core idea that the *target itself*, not just the data distribution, should differ by $\beta$. Retraining while increasing the auxiliary strength $\lambda$ 15-fold (0.01 $\to$ 0.15), the Spearman rank correlation between $\beta$ order and mean cash-weight order improves monotonically: $+0.400$ (original v3, the wrong direction) $\to$ $-0.400$ ($\lambda=0.01$) $\to$ $-0.800$ ($\lambda=0.15$). The absolute effect is small, however — even at $\lambda=0.15$ the per-$\beta$ mean cash weights range only from 0.98% to 3.38%, all near the uniform initialization ($1/45\approx2.2\%$); the ranks line up in the intended direction, but behavior does not meaningfully diverge. Even so, this dose-response relationship — obtained by manipulating a single variable (signal strength) and observing a response in the predicted direction — confirms that the collapse is controllable via the diagnosed mechanism (objective-level signal vs. sampling distribution) rather than an artifact of a single run or an implementation-specific tuning failure, and is consistent with the objective-level diversity-injection principle established by FeUdal Networks and DIAYN (Eysenbach et al., 2019; Section 2.2). Restored diversity does not, however, guarantee improved performance: evaluating the $\lambda=0.15$ pool with the same inference-time smoothing as Section 4.2 shows all four specialists performing near equal-weight on validation, and the candidate with the flashiest raw test return ($\beta=-30$, +8.64%) has the worst validation/test Sharpe gap of the four (0.850) — the same overfitting pattern confirmed in Section 4.6, reproduced here in this reduced-scope experiment.

### 4.4 v4 — Restoring diversity with a structural action-space change

Rather than attempt to reintroduce a DP-style teacher (intractable at this scale and asset count — see Section 8), we instead change the shape of the action space itself, adapting the two mechanisms described in Section 2.2. `SparseGatedCryptoPortfolioEnv` takes a $(N{+}1)$-dimensional action: the first $N$ components are per-asset confidence logits, and the last is a single risk-gate logit. The transform first selects the top-$K$ ($K=10$; held fixed throughout Section 4, swept post hoc in Section 6.8) assets by confidence logit and applies softmax within that subset only (adapting FreQuant's Eq. 13–14), then scales the resulting sub-portfolio by $\sigma(\text{gate logit}) \in (0,1)$ (adapting DeepClair's exposure gate $\rho$); the remainder of the portfolio is implicitly held in cash. Stage A's regime chunks and $\beta$-conditioned sampling priority are reused unchanged from v1, and the specialist and router training budgets are held identical to v1 (Section 3.3), so that the only variable changed relative to v1 is the shape of the action space.

A three-part diversity diagnostic — the time-series standard deviation of the risk gate $\rho$, the mean $\rho$ conditioned on $\beta$, and the number of distinct assets appearing in any step's top-10 holdings over the test window — is run before evaluating performance, specifically to check whether v3's collapse reproduces. It does not: $\rho$'s standard deviation is 0.068 (effectively zero in v3), mean $\rho$, measured as in the original diagnostic on a single training chunk, ranges from 0.109 ($\beta=-30$) to 0.545 ($\beta=90$), and the top-10 holdings rotate through 32 of the 44 available assets over the test window (21–27 when each $\beta$ runs alone). Because the original diagnostic looks at one 76-step chunk, the re-run also measured it as an average over 20 training chunks (0.089–0.274) and over a standalone run of the full test window (0.057–0.670). In all three measurements $\beta=90$ has the highest $\rho$, so separation by risk preference holds, but the order of the remaining $\beta$ values changes across measurements and is not monotonic. Diversity is structurally restored. Performance, however, is mixed: the *raw* (unsmoothed) policy pool has an essentially zero pre-cost alpha (−0.62%), meaning the low-level sparse-and-gated policy alone is weaker than v1's dense policy before any smoothing is applied. Applying the same $\alpha=0.01$ inference-time smoothing from Section 4.2 yields +9.37% net of costs, Sharpe 2.314, MDD −5.01% — lower total return than v1+smoothing (+11.14%, Sharpe 0.637, MDD −37.17%) but a markedly better risk-adjusted profile (this $\alpha$, too, was chosen on test). Unlike v1 (Section 4.2), where pre-cost alpha was positive for every $\alpha$ and nearly the same at $\alpha=1$ and $\alpha=0.01$ (11.98%, 12.62%), v4's pre-cost alpha itself rises monotonically as $\alpha$ falls (from −0.62% at $\alpha=1$ to +10.06% at $\alpha=0.01$). Inference-time smoothing is therefore not merely preserving a fixed pre-existing signal here, as it does for v1 — it changes which trades are realized in a way that produces a positive pre-cost return where the unsmoothed policy has essentially none. We do not have a mechanistic account of why this particular smoothing filter recovers a pre-cost edge from what looks like near-noise in the raw policy, and report this as an open question rather than a demonstrated causal story (Section 6.8's near-zero rank correlation between the top-$K$ confidence signal and realized returns is a related piece of the same puzzle, discovered independently). A separate diagnostic rules out that this low-turnover result is a static-position artifact (turnover still averages 0.10% per step over the last 10% of the test window rather than decaying to zero; the first and second halves of the window both contribute positive return, +5.55% and +3.62%; and the Jaccard similarity between the asset sets selected in the first and last quarters is 0.26, so holdings genuinely rotate — though the front-loading is noted as an open concern in Section 8).

### 4.5 The "matched" router and its collapse

The router in Section 4.4 observes, among other macro features, the fraction of the current regime chunk elapsed (`episode_progress`) — a quantity that is well-defined during training, where each chunk is a bounded episode, but has no natural definition at live-inference time, where there is no episode boundary; the deployed system approximates it with a constant 0.5. To remove this train/serve mismatch, we retrain only Stage C (the router) with `episode_progress` fixed to 0.5 during training as well, reusing the unchanged Stage B specialist pool from Section 4.4 — a "matched" router. A single run of this matched system appears to improve on Section 4.4's already-strong result: net return moves from +9.37% to +8.03% (a small decrease) while Sharpe rises from 2.314 to 3.033 and MDD improves from −5.01% to −3.36%. At this point in the investigation this looked like a successful fix for a genuine train/serve inconsistency.

A follow-up ablation — designed to check *why* the fix helped, rather than simply accepting that it did — instead shows that it did not help at all. Inspecting the matched router's selection log shows it selects the $\beta=30$ specialist on 100% of both validation-period and test-period chunks (195/195 test chunks; 92/92 validation chunks in the original, pre-correction run — see Section 4.6). Running the $\beta=30$ specialist alone, with no router, reproduces the matched-router test numbers exactly (+8.03%, Sharpe 3.033, MDD −3.36% both ways; in the original run the validation numbers also matched to four decimal places). The router had not learned to route market-conditionally; matching the training and serving distributions of `episode_progress` had, in this instance, caused Stage C's learning process to collapse onto a single constant action, discarding all of its macro inputs. The apparent Sharpe/MDD improvement reported above was not a property of the router's decision-making — it was a property of the $\beta=30$ specialist alone, which the previous (unmatched) router had also been selecting on a plurality, but not entirety, of test-period chunks.

### 4.6 Which specialist, and is its test performance real?

This discovery reframes the remaining question: is the $\beta=30$ specialist's standalone performance — inherited unchanged, it turns out, from the collapsed router — actually good, or does it merely look good on this particular test window? We answer this by running all four Stage-B specialists standalone (no router at all) over both the validation period (2025 Q4) and the test period (2026 H1):

| $\beta$ | Validation Sharpe | Test Sharpe | Validation return | Test return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| −90 | −0.373 | 0.471 | −4.63% | +3.58% |
| **−30** | **0.914** | **1.310** | **+3.02%** | **+6.66%** |
| 30 | 0.189 | 3.033 | +0.44% | +8.03% |
| 90 | −1.284 | −1.175 | −20.75% | −24.68% |
| Equal-weight | −0.459 | 0.255 | −32.56% | −0.81% |

The four specialists behave differently across the two periods. $\beta=90$ fails in both; $\beta=-90$ loses in validation (−4.63%) and gains in test (+3.58%); only $\beta=-30$ is positive in both. $\beta=30$ jumps from a validation Sharpe of 0.189 to the highest test Sharpe of any specialist (3.033). The validation period (2025 Q4) is a sell-off in which equal weight returned −32.56%, while the test period is roughly flat for equal weight, so there is a regime difference that favors every specialist in test. But the improvement is unusually large for $\beta=30$ (+2.84 Sharpe, versus +0.11 to +0.84 for the others). The conservative reading is that $\beta=30$'s test advantage is specific to this test window rather than an edge verifiable on validation.

**Selection rule.** We deploy the specialist with the highest validation-period Sharpe ratio. This rule uses no test information and selects $\beta=-30$ (validation Sharpe 0.914; runner-up $\beta=30$ at 0.189). An earlier version of this paper described the validation-to-test Sharpe gap as the selection criterion; because computing the gap requires test performance, that amounted to using test information for selection. This version reports the gap only as a post-hoc diagnostic: 0.844 for $\beta=-90$, **0.396 for $\beta=-30$**, 2.844 for $\beta=30$, 0.109 for $\beta=90$. The smallest gap belongs to $\beta=90$, which loses money in both periods — a small gap alone is not evidence of a good policy; it is meaningful only in combination with positive performance. The smoothing coefficient $\alpha=0.01$ was first fixed on test results in v1, but re-selecting it on validation for the matched-router pool (Section 4.5) and for the clean retrain (Section 6.3) gives the same value. The final deployed policy is therefore the $\beta=-30$ specialist alone, with no router, combined with $\alpha=0.01$ inference-time smoothing — the simplest configuration in the cascade, reached only after ablations showed that each more complex configuration (v1's router, v3's cash-augmented selection, v4's matched router) was not doing what its surface metrics suggested. This selection rests on a single validation window (4,416 steps) and a single seed, and Section 6 shows that other seeds of the same configuration do not reproduce its validation performance.

## 6. Robustness Analysis

Having selected $\beta=-30$ on the basis of validation-period Sharpe (Section 4.6), we treat that selection itself as a claim requiring independent verification. Except for Section 6.8, every figure is recomputed with the corrected accounting of Section 3.3.

### 6.1 Multi-seed reproducibility

We retrain the deployed configuration ($\beta=-30$, top-$K$+gate specialist, $\alpha=0.01$ smoothing, no router) with five additional random seeds (11, 22, 33, 44, 55), holding the architecture and training budget fixed:

| Seed | Validation return | Validation Sharpe | Test return | Test Sharpe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **42 (deployed)** | +3.02% | **0.914** | +6.66% | 1.310 |
| 11 | −8.00% | −0.427 | +41.50% | 2.577 |
| 22 | +0.77% | 0.253 | +4.12% | 0.902 |
| 33 | −3.96% | −0.347 | −0.53% | −0.008 |
| 44 | −11.64% | −1.355 | +9.27% | 1.142 |
| 55 | −4.36% | −0.880 | +5.24% | 1.107 |
| Equal-weight | −32.56% | −0.459 | −0.81% | 0.255 |

**Direction mostly reproduces in test**: five of six seeds have higher test Sharpe than equal weight (0.255); only seed 33 falls short (−0.008). **It does not reproduce in validation**: only seeds 42 and 22 have positive validation Sharpe, and seeds 44 and 55 are below even equal weight's validation Sharpe (−0.459), although by return every seed loses less than equal weight's −32.56%. **Magnitude does not reproduce either**: the five new seeds' test Sharpe averages 1.144 with standard deviation 0.927. Seeds 11 and 44 show a pattern similar to $\beta=30$ in Section 4.6 — negative in validation, high Sharpe in test (−0.427→2.577 and −1.355→1.142). Seed 42's validation/test gap (0.396) is the second smallest of the six; the smallest, seed 33 (0.339), is small only because both periods are weak. Positive performance in both periods is therefore a property of seed 42 (and, weakly, seed 22), not of the configuration in general.

To address the concern that six seeds is a thin sample, we extend the sweep with ten further seeds (66, 77, 88, 99, 110, 121, 132, 143, 154, 165) to $N=16$. Only one of the ten new seeds (165, 0.215) has positive validation Sharpe, while nine of ten have positive test Sharpe (only seed 66 is negative, −0.579). Four new seeds fall short of equal weight's test Sharpe (0.255): 66, 77 (0.112), 99 (0.142), and 121 (0.167). Across all 16 seeds, 14 have positive test Sharpe and 11 beat equal weight in test, but only 3 (42, 22, 165) have positive validation Sharpe. The 15 non-deployed seeds' test Sharpe averages 0.823 with standard deviation 0.858. Seed 42 still has the highest validation Sharpe of all 16 candidates (0.914; second is seed 22 at 0.253). This means validation-Sharpe selection would have picked the same seed, but also that its validation result is the most extreme of the 16 — picking the validation maximum among many candidates itself introduces selection bias.

We read this as evidence that, because the validation period (2025 Q4, an equal-weight sell-off of −32.56%) and the test period (2026 H1, roughly flat for equal weight) differ sharply in regime, this configuration's policies tend to lose in the sell-off and gain in the flat period. A single seed's two-period consistency is not enough to claim robustness beyond this regime difference.

### 6.2 Deflated Sharpe Ratio

We apply the Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014; Section 2.5) to the seed sweep, treating each seed as one trial in the multiple-comparison correction, and compare the deployed seed (42) with the seed of highest raw test Sharpe (11):

| Candidate | Test Sharpe | DSR ($N=6$) | DSR ($N=16$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seed 42 (deployed) | 1.310 | 0.566 | 0.442 |
| Seed 11 (raw maximum) | 2.577 | 0.861 | 0.781 |

Two findings run counter to an initial, intuitive expectation. First, we had expected the seed with the highest raw Sharpe (11) to be penalized *more* by the multiple-comparison correction, on the logic that the maximum of many trials is the least trustworthy — yet its DSR is higher than the deployed seed's under both settings. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio's threshold $SR^*$ is a function of the *number of trials* and applies equally to every candidate drawn from that set; DSR measures whether each candidate clears this common, trial-count-dependent threshold, not whether it was the maximum. A candidate with higher raw Sharpe clears the common threshold more easily, so multiple-comparison correction and "distrust the maximum" are not the same operation. Second, extending $N$ from 6 to 16 raises $SR^*$ and lowers both candidates' DSR as expected (0.566→0.442, 0.861→0.781), but the qualitative conclusion is unchanged: no candidate clears the conventional 0.95 significance level at either sample size. The deployed seed's case rests on validation performance, not DSR significance, and, as Section 6.1 shows, that case itself rests on a single seed.

Following Section 2.5, we emphasize that the Deflated Sharpe Ratio and the validation/test Sharpe gap answer different questions: DSR asks whether the best of $N$ trials *within* a single test period survives correction for that selection; the validation/test gap asks whether a policy's quality holds when *the evaluation period itself* changes. A policy can pass one and fail the other — seed 11 has the highest test-period DSR but loses money in validation. Neither diagnostic subsumes the other, so we report both.

### 6.3 Leakage-free clean-validation retraining

The choices of $\alpha$ and of the $\beta$ specialist were made on a validation period (2025 Q4) that overlaps the training period (all of 2025), raising the concern that "validation" was not fully held out in the conventional sense. To check this directly, we retrain $\beta=-30$ (seed 42) on **only** January–September 2025 (the same boundary as the v3 experiment in Section 4.3), sweep $\alpha$ on the genuinely unseen October–December 2025 window, and run the resulting configuration exactly once on the 2026 H1 test window:

| | Validation return | Validation Sharpe | Test return | Test Sharpe | Test MDD |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equal-weight benchmark | −32.56% | −0.459 | −0.81% | 0.255 | −40.46% |
| Deployed (trained on all of 2025, selected on the overlapping validation window) | +3.02% | 0.914 | +6.66% | 1.310 | −7.35% |
| **Clean retrain (trained on 9 months, $\alpha$ selected on the genuinely unseen 3 months)** | −3.48% | −0.573 | **+6.39%** | **1.110** | −8.63% |

The clean retrain loses money in validation for every $\alpha$ (from −15.02% at $\alpha=1.0$ to −3.48% at $\alpha=0.01$, the smallest loss, which is selected) and performs similarly to the deployed model in test (+6.39%, Sharpe 1.110). Two readings follow. First, the similar test performance is evidence that exposing the validation window during training did not substantially inflate the deployed model's *test* performance. Second, the deployed model's positive *validation* result (+3.02%) may be due to having trained on that window — a model that never saw it loses money there. The deployed model's validation figures should therefore not be read as an independent holdout result. We do not switch deployment to the clean retrain — switching based on test results would reintroduce test-set-informed selection.

### 6.4 Ensemble averaging (a negative result)

A natural way to reduce the seed-to-seed variance documented in Section 6.1 is to average several seeds' portfolio weights at every step instead of deploying a single seed. We test this with no further training, using the original six seeds (42 plus the five from Section 6.1):

| Configuration | Validation Sharpe | Test Sharpe | \|Valid − Test\| gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seed 33 | −0.347 | −0.008 | 0.339 (smallest, but negative in both) |
| **Seed 42 (deployed)** | **0.914** | **1.310** | **0.396** |
| Seed 22 | 0.253 | 0.902 | 0.649 |
| Ensemble of 5 (excluding seed 11) | −0.460 | 0.957 | 1.417 |
| Seed 55 | −0.880 | 1.107 | 1.988 |
| Ensemble of all 6 | −0.464 | 1.644 | 2.108 |
| Seed 44 | −1.355 | 1.142 | 2.497 |
| Seed 11 | −0.427 | 2.577 | 3.003 |

Both ensembles have high test Sharpe (1.644 for all six) but negative validation Sharpe, and a much larger validation/test gap than the deployed seed — the full ensemble's gap (2.108) is about five times seed 42's (0.396). Because most seeds lose money in validation, averaging their weights appears to follow the majority's behavior. We expected averaging independently trained seeds to average away noise and reduce variance; in this setting it does not improve validation performance. One plausible explanation we did not investigate further is that the specialists' top-$K$ selections differ enough across seeds that averaging in weight space dilutes each seed's sparse, concentrated positions — an untested hypothesis, not a confirmed mechanism. The practical conclusion is clear: **weight-averaging seed ensembles do not improve two-period consistency in this setting.**

### 6.5 Classical baselines

We compare the deployed policy against four baselines that involve no learning: buy-and-hold (no rebalancing after the initial allocation, buying only assets tradable at the start), cross-sectional momentum (daily rebalancing into the top-10 assets by trailing 7-day return), Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage mean-variance optimization (weekly rebalancing, unconstrained global minimum variance, no short sales), and risk parity (weekly rebalancing in proportion to the inverse of trailing 90-day volatility). Positions are held between rebalancing dates and drift with prices. Results are reported in Table 2 (Section 5) and repeated here for discussion: momentum achieves the highest raw test return in the study (+97.41%) but loses money in validation (−18.21%), with the largest validation/test Sharpe gap among baselines (2.490). MVO fails outright, doing worse than equal weight in both periods (test −6.96%, validation −39.16%) — we attribute this negative result to the high pairwise correlations typical of crypto assets, which leave covariance-based optimization little structure to exploit. Risk parity fails in the same direction (test −15.11%, validation −38.84%). Of the six strategies compared (equal weight, buy-and-hold, momentum, MVO, risk parity, and the deployed RL policy), only the deployed policy has a positive Sharpe ratio in both the validation and test periods. The counterexample to "a small validation/test gap is itself evidence of a good policy" comes not from this table but from the $\beta=90$ specialist in Section 4.6 (gap 0.109, losses in both periods) — the gap is meaningful only in combination with positive raw performance.

### 6.6 A deep-learning baseline: EIIE

To rule out that the RL policy's advantage over classical baselines merely reflects the value of *any* portfolio-management neural network, we implement EIIE (Jiang, Xu, & Liang, 2017) — the network topology of a shared per-asset CNN evaluator, a portfolio-vector memory, and a cash bias, producing softmax portfolio weights — trained with this paper's PPO pipeline rather than the original paper's online stochastic batch learning (OSBL) procedure, using raw OHLC price windows with no embedding backbone, under the same bucket structure, transaction cost, and 100k-step training budget as $\beta=-30$. EIIE scores 0.176 test Sharpe, close to the equal-weight benchmark recomputed in this notebook (0.214), and similarly close to equal weight in validation (−0.990 vs. −1.000$^\ddagger$) — i.e., **EIIE converges to an equal-weight-like policy**, reproducing the same near-uniform collapse observed in Section 4.3's v3 experiment, in an entirely different network architecture and action parameterization. This strengthens the Section 4.3 diagnosis: the difficulty in this setting is not specific to the sparse-selection or dense-softmax action spaces used elsewhere in this paper, but appears to be a more general property of training a portfolio-weighting policy from scratch, without an external diversity-inducing signal, on this asset universe and data budget. The deployed $\beta=-30$ policy outperforms EIIE by a wide margin in test Sharpe (1.310 vs. 0.176), which we interpret as evidence that the combination of a pretrained embedding backbone (Section 3.2), structural action-space sparsification (Section 4.4), and inference-time smoothing (Section 4.2) is doing real work relative to a standard deep RL portfolio baseline, rather than simply benefiting from a stronger PPO implementation.

Since EIIE (2017) is now a relatively old point in the deep-learning portfolio-management literature, we implement a more recent attention-based architecture the same way: **LSRE-CAAN** (Li, Zhang, Yang, & Chen, 2023) — an attention-bottleneck encoder (LSRE) in which a small number of learned latents cross-attend to the price sequence, followed by a module (CAAN) that applies cross-asset attention over the resulting per-asset representations to produce final scores — trained with the same PPO pipeline and the same raw price-window input. LSRE-CAAN scores 0.214 test Sharpe, essentially the same as its notebook's equal-weight benchmark (0.214) — a **third architecture (the 45-dimensional dense-softmax v3, the CNN-based EIIE, and the attention-based LSRE-CAAN) reproducing the same near-uniform collapse**. This further strengthens the Section 4.3/6.6 diagnosis: the diversity failure in this setting is not a product of any particular network topology, but appears to be a general property of training a portfolio-weighting policy from scratch without an external diversity-inducing signal (such as EarnHFT's Q-teacher). The deployed $\beta=-30$ policy outperforms LSRE-CAAN by a wide margin in test Sharpe (1.310 vs. 0.214). As Section 6.1 shows, this compares against a single deployed seed; the advantage holds against the seed mean (1.144) too, but some seeds, such as seed 33, fall short of equal weight.

$^\ddagger$ See the Table 2 footnote (Section 5) regarding this notebook's independently constructed evaluation buckets.

### 6.7 Auxiliary evidence: restoring the Q-teacher restores diversity, on a single asset

Section 4.3 attributes v3's diversity collapse to the absence of EarnHFT's Stage I dynamic-programming teacher, which is intractable to reintroduce directly at the 44-asset, continuous-weight scale studied in this paper (Section 8). As an indirect check on this causal claim, we note results from a parallel, single-asset branch of this project that reimplements EarnHFT closer to its original scope — Bitcoin only, discrete long/flat/short positions, including Stage I. In this setting, where a DP-optimal teacher *is* tractable, the initial version (v1, later found to have a NAV accounting bug) showed per-$\beta$ average positions separated from 0.39 to 0.79 — diversity by the same measure that collapsed in Section 4.3's 44-asset, no-teacher experiment. In the final version with the accounting bug fixed, however, the five $\beta$ values (−150, −90, −10, 30, 100) show average positions of only 0.42–0.66, and the ordering is not monotone in $\beta$ ($\beta=-10$ at 0.651 exceeds $\beta=30$ at 0.485). This branch is therefore only weak qualitative evidence that a Q-teacher produces $\beta$-dependent behavior, not a strong confirmation of the Section 4.3 diagnosis. This single-asset branch's overall returns are poor for unrelated reasons (an initial NAV accounting bug, and a rule-based router mis-calibrated to the wrong decision boundary), and its results are not otherwise comparable to the 44-asset results in this paper — we report only the diversity outcome, as corroborating evidence for the causal role of Stage I identified in Section 4.3, not as an additional performance result.

### 6.8 Top-$K$ hyperparameter sensitivity sweep

Section 4.4 fixed top-$K$ at $K=10$ for the entire v1–v4 comparison (part of the general policy, described in Section 3.3, of holding budgets equal across configurations). That this choice was made without a sweep was originally flagged as a limitation (Section 8). **All figures in this section (including the $K=10$ row) come from the pre-correction environment of Section 3.3 and were not recomputed; the $K=10$ row keeps its pre-correction value so that like is compared with like.** To check it post hoc, we hold the deployed configuration fixed ($\beta=-30$, $\alpha=0.01$ smoothing, no router) and retrain with $K \in \{5, 7, 15\}$ (reusing the Section 4.4 model for $K=10$), applying the same validation (2025 Q4) / test (2026 H1) consistency diagnostic used in Section 4.6:

| $K$ | Val. Sharpe | Test Sharpe | \|Val.−Test\| gap | Val. return | Test return |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 1.741 | 0.541 | 1.200 | +4.45% | +1.72% |
| 7 | −0.516 | 1.593 | 2.109 | −7.85% | +18.51% |
| **10 (deployed)** | **1.314** | 1.156 | **0.158** | +4.54% | +5.66% |
| 15 | −0.200 | 0.734 | 0.934 | −3.76% | +8.07% |

Of the four swept values, only the deployed $K=10$ achieves a validation/test gap below 0.2; the other three all exceed 0.9. $K=7$ in particular reproduces exactly the pattern seen with $\beta=30$ in Section 4.6: it fails outright in validation (Sharpe $-0.516$, return $-7.85\%$) yet posts the second-highest raw test return in the entire study (+18.51%, more than triple $K{=}10$'s). Applying Section 4.6's reasoning directly, this jump should be read as test-period-specific overfitting rather than a verifiable edge — choosing $K=7$ on raw test return alone would have repeated the exact mistake avoided when the $\beta=30$ specialist was rejected. **$K=10$ is therefore not an untuned, arbitrary choice, but the only value among those swept that passes this paper's consistency criterion** — though the swept range ($\{5,7,10,15\}$) does not exactly match the range originally flagged as a future candidate ($\{5,10,15,20\}$) in Section 8.

This sweep also surfaces an incidental negative result. Measuring the Spearman rank correlation (IC) between each asset's confidence logit — the signal used for top-$K$ selection — and its realized return, across all four $\beta$ specialists on the test period, yields mean IC of $0.0006$–$0.0057$ (indistinguishable from zero), a share of IC-positive steps of $50.3$–$51.5\%$ (indistinguishable from a coin flip), and no statistically significant IC for any $\beta$ except $-90$ ($t=3.33$). In other words, **there is little evidence that the confidence logits actually predict the cross-sectional ranking of next-period returns** — performance differences across $K$ are more plausibly attributable to how the number of exposed assets interacts with the risk gate $\rho$'s effective concentration (Section 4.4) than to accurate stock selection. We report this as a reassessment of the top-$K$ mechanism's value, separate from the performance result above. This connects to an open question flagged in Section 4.4: the smoothed deployed policy's own pre-cost return is positive despite this near-zero stock-selection signal, and we do not have a mechanistic account of why.

| 10bp (deployed) | 0.914 | +3.02% | 1.310 | +6.66% |
| 15bp | 0.854 | +2.80% | 1.211 | +6.13% |
| 20bp | 0.794 | +2.58% | 1.113 | +5.59% |

Swinging costs from 0.5x to 2x still beats the equal-weight benchmark (test Sharpe 0.255, validation Sharpe −0.459) in both periods — even at 2x cost (20bp), test Sharpe is 1.113, more than 4x the benchmark. The deployed policy's turnover is low (about 0.11% per step), so even at 20bp the cost drag on its pre-cost test return (+7.74%) is only 2.0%. The conclusion for the deployed policy survives a materially wrong $\tau$ assumption. This sweep concerns the deployed seed only and does not reduce the seed-to-seed variation of Section 6.1.

### 6.10 A formal hypothesis test for the validation/test gap diagnostic

As noted in Section 2.5, the validation/test Sharpe-gap diagnostic used throughout Section 4.6 has so far been a qualitative threshold comparison. We formalize it, in the spirit of Gort et al.'s (2022) hypothesis-testing formulation, as a **block permutation test**: preserving short-horizon return autocorrelation with one-day (48-step) blocks, we compute the probability, under the null hypothesis that validation and test period labels are exchangeable, of observing a $|$validation Sharpe $-$ test Sharpe$|$ at least as large as the one actually observed. We apply this to all four $\beta$ specialists:

| $\beta$ | Gap (test $-$ valid) | Permutation $p$-value |
|---|---|---|
| $-90$ | $+0.844$ | $0.700$ |
| $-30$ (deployed) | $+0.396$ | $0.870$ |
| $30$ | $+2.844$ | $\mathbf{0.353}$ |
| $90$ | $+0.109$ | $0.954$ |

$\beta=30$ has the smallest $p$-value of the four (0.353 vs. 0.70–0.95 for the rest) — consistent in direction with the qualitative judgment in Section 4.6. It falls well short of a conventional significance threshold ($p<0.05$), however — exactly the pattern observed for the Deflated Sharpe Ratio in Section 6.2: the direction is right, but formal statistical significance is not reached. We attribute this low power to the test's own conservatism rather than to a weak underlying effect: preserving day-level autocorrelation forces permutation at the granularity of ~194 one-day blocks rather than the raw 9,319 steps, and daily crypto Sharpe ratios are themselves high-variance, so a two-sided test with only four $\beta$ candidates compared has limited ability to reach $p<0.05$ on a single test window even when the underlying gap is real — the same limitation, driven by short sample windows, noted for walk-forward validation in Limitation 5. So even $\beta=30$'s large gap is not distinguishable from chance by this test, and for the same reason the deployed policy's small gap is not statistical evidence of consistency. The deployment argument does not rest on this permutation test achieving formal statistical significance (Section 8, Limitation 6).

### 6.11 Exposure-matched benchmarks: where does the low drawdown come from?

Because of the risk gate $\rho$ and inertial smoothing, the deployed policy keeps only part of its capital in risky assets on average (realized exposure: 16.6% in validation, 18.4% in test). To check whether its drawdown advantage over 100%-invested equal weight is due to this low exposure, we compute two exposure-matched benchmarks with the same environment and accounting: "fixed exposure", which invests the policy's average exposure in equal weight, and "policy exposure path", which holds equal weight at the policy's exposure at every step.

| | Val. return | Val. Sharpe | Val. MDD | Test return | Test Sharpe | Test MDD |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\beta=-30$ deployed policy | +3.02% | 0.914 | −6.20% | +6.66% | 1.310 | −7.35% |
| Equal weight, 100% | −32.56% | −0.459 | −43.82% | −0.81% | 0.255 | −40.46% |
| Equal weight × fixed exposure | −3.38% | −0.488 | −7.97% | +0.94% | 0.221 | −8.85% |
| Equal weight × policy exposure path | +0.12% | 0.113 | −8.44% | +6.45% | 1.207 | −8.28% |

Three things follow. First, **most of the drawdown improvement comes from lower exposure**: simply holding equal weight along the policy's exposure path gives a test MDD of −8.28%, close to the policy's −7.35%. The "82% reduction" in contribution 4 (Section 1.4) is relative to 100%-invested equal weight; relative to matched exposure the improvement is small. Second, **most of the test-period performance comes from exposure timing**: equal weight along the policy's exposure path reaches test Sharpe 1.207, far above fixed exposure (0.221) and close to the policy (1.310). In the test period, when exposure is raised and lowered (the risk gate plus smoothing) matters more than which assets are selected — consistent with Section 6.8's finding that top-$K$ confidence has near-zero rank correlation with realized returns. Third, **asset selection matters more in validation**: equal weight along the same exposure path reaches only 0.113 validation Sharpe versus the policy's 0.914. But as Section 6.3 shows, the deployed model was trained on the validation window, so this validation-period selection effect should not be read as out-of-sample evidence.

## 7. Multi-Market Analysis

(All figures in this section are computed under the corrected environment of Section 3.3. The KOSPI single-policy PPO was retrained in the corrected environment; the KOSPI and U.S. large-cap HRL pools, the two U.S. single policies, and the point-in-time experiment reuse policies trained under the pre-correction environment and were re-evaluated in the corrected one (Limitation 11). KOSPI and U.S. large-caps have no pre-listing or post-delisting assets, so only the holding-drift correction applies to them.) Section 1.1 opened with a cross-market observation: two experiments with unrelated data pipelines, state designs, and action spaces (Korean equities, cryptocurrency) both appeared to show a single-policy PPO agent failing to beat its equal-weight benchmark. Here we revisit that observation (the Korean side was overturned by a rerun with pipeline defects fixed), then report a **third, independent market check (U.S. large-cap equities)** carried out later in this project, and finally synthesize all three to state precisely what they do, and do not, establish about the generality of this paper's findings.

**The Korean-equity rerun: the single-policy failure was a pipeline defect.** The KOSPI observation in Section 1.1 (+0.28% vs. equal-weight +34.33%) turned out to be unreliable on audit. With identical code and seed, swapping only the backbone to Chronos+iTransformer gave +137.22% (Sharpe 2.473); the embedding extraction skipped RevIN normalization and fed features in a different order than in training; and the data split let both the DTW clustering window (2022–2024) and the backbone training window (through 2024-06) overlap the 2024–2025 test period. With all four fixed (clustering on 2021–2023, backbone training through 2023-06, RevIN applied, feature order matched), we retrained the single-policy PPO on two backbones × five seeds (`kospi_rerun_colab.ipynb`). The result reverses the original observation. Nine of ten runs beat equal-weight's test Sharpe (Chronos+iTransformer backbone +44.2%±9.3% / Sharpe 1.09±0.22, 4 of 5 runs; Chronos+MTGNN backbone +41.7%±2.6% / 1.07±0.09, 5 of 5; equal-weight +31.0% / 0.943), and all ten beat it on return, although seven of ten had a deeper maximum drawdown than equal-weight (−16.1%). We also checked where the edge comes from (seed 42, both backbones). A static portfolio holding the training-period average cluster weights throughout the test period performs almost identically to PPO (Sharpe 1.108 vs. PPO 1.134; 1.099 vs. 1.168 for the MTGNN backbone), and holding the test-period average weights closes the gap to within 0.01. The MTGNN-backbone policy keeps about 96% in a single cluster for the whole test period (time standard deviation of its weight 0.006), and holding that cluster alone (Sharpe 1.185) beats the ten-run PPO mean. The corrected KOSPI single policy therefore beats equal-weight, but through a static cluster tilt fixed during training rather than through period-by-period decisions. That both backbones' validation directional accuracy (0.495 / 0.515) falls below the validation period's majority-direction rate (0.533) is consistent with the embeddings carrying almost no timing information. The claim motivating this paper's entire architectural investigation — **single-policy RL using a pretrained embedding-based state and a dense action space is not a reliable starting point for continuous portfolio allocation** — now rests only on the crypto observation and the U.S. large-cap controlled experiment below. The Korean result neither supports nor refutes it (it beats equal-weight, but the embeddings do almost nothing).

**A third market complicates the claim.** We checked the same question (does single-policy PPO beat equal-weight?) on 44 U.S. large-cap stocks (11 GICS sectors, 2015–2025, `us_equity_hrl_generalization_colab.ipynb`). Rather than building a new embedding backbone — itself a months-scale undertaking — this check reuses the raw price-window state representation already validated by the Section 6.6 EIIE baseline (no embeddings). That is, this check varies the state representation along with the market. Result: single-policy PPO finished the 2024–2025 test window essentially tied with equal-weight (+45.58%/Sharpe 1.482 vs. +45.00%/Sharpe 1.454) — the dramatic failure seen on crypto does not reproduce. As already noted in Section 1.1, this is the second time this pattern appears in this project's own data: "failure reproduced" (the original crypto agent) is embedding-based, and "failure not reproduced" (crypto EIIE, U.S. equities) is raw-price-window-based in both cases.

**A follow-up that directly controls the confound.** Because market and architecture were fully confounded above, we ran a further check on the same U.S. large-cap market, the same 44 stocks, and the same 2024–2025 test window, this time equipping the state with Chronos zero-shot embeddings (`us_equity_embedding_confound_colab.ipynb`, skipping the MTGNN training stage and using only the pretrained Chronos encoder at inference time — one of the two components of the original hybrid backbone from Section 3.2 — with its 512-dimensional output reduced to 64 dimensions by PCA) — i.e., the same family of state representation used for Korean equities and crypto. This holds the market fixed and varies only the architecture. Result: performance moved from "tied" to "clearly behind" equal-weight (test Sharpe 1.482 → 1.251, against equal-weight's 1.458; the two notebooks compute equal-weight independently, at 1.454 and 1.458, so relative to each notebook's own equal-weight the two arms are +0.028 and −0.207), accompanied by a roughly 8x rise in average turnover (1.22% to 9.2–9.5% per step; before the accounting correction this read as 24x from 0.38%, because the error of Section 3.3 left rebalancing out of the raw-price policy's measured turnover) — the same mechanism documented for v1 in Section 4.1 ("turnover erodes alpha"). **Architecture is a genuine causal factor** in the failure — though not as catastrophic as on crypto (Sharpe −4.134). We read this as architecture explaining the *direction* of the failure (why it occurs — turnover instability induced by an embedding-based dense action space) and market or regime modulating its *severity* — not a either/or explanation, but both factors operating together. We flag one caveat on the strength of this causal claim: unlike the deployed policy (validated across 16 seeds, Section 6.1), this confound-controlling comparison is a single training run in each arm, with no seed-robustness check of its own. The two pieces of evidence differ in how exposed they are to this gap: the roughly eightfold turnover rise is a large, qualitative shift, mechanistically consistent with the cost-erosion pattern documented for v1 (Section 4.1), and not plausibly an artifact of run-to-run noise at this scale; the smaller Sharpe change (1.482→1.251) is harder to rule out as noise on its own, given that seed-to-seed test Sharpe variation of comparable magnitude has been observed elsewhere in this paper (Section 6.1: $\sigma=0.927$ across five additional seeds of crypto's $\beta=-30$ specialist, albeit for a different architecture and market, so not a direct bound on noise here). The controlled design (market, universe, test window, environment, and reward held fixed, with only the state representation varied) supports a causal reading in principle, but we cannot yet rule out that a portion of the observed Sharpe gap specifically is run-to-run noise rather than an architecture effect; repeating both arms across multiple seeds is the natural next step to firm up this claim (Section 8, Limitation 1).

**What one market does not support.** The core structural interventions in the rest of this paper — the structural fix via sparse action spaces (Section 4.4) and the router's collapse into a constant function (Section 4.5) — remain crypto-only: their motivation is specific to the 44-asset dense action space, and, as described below, we do not port them to the 5-dimensional KOSPI action space. The Q-teacher-shaped diversity gap (Section 4.3) and the validation/test consistency diagnostic (Section 4.6) are partially tested by the reduced-scope KOSPI experiment described below. The KOSPI track originally concluded, after its single-policy failure (shown above to be a pipeline artifact), with a simpler daily-rebalancing PPO baseline (documented in the project's supplementary technical log, outside this paper's scope) rather than an equivalent hierarchical redesign.

Before reporting this reduced-scope test, one framing issue needs to be resolved first: EarnHFT's core motivation (Section 2.1) is the transaction-cost drag that compounds under second-level rebalancing, and Sections 4.1–4.2's crypto v1 experiment reproduces and fixes exactly that problem. KOSPI, however, rebalances daily — a cadence at which the problem EarnHFT was designed to solve has essentially no room to arise, since a once-a-day policy cannot accumulate the kind of compounding cost drag documented in Section 4.1. The KOSPI test below should therefore not be read as asking whether EarnHFT's core high-frequency mechanism also works in a slower market; read that way, the comparison is timescale-mismatched from the start. What it can legitimately test is a narrower, separate hypothesis this paper's own evidence already supports: the confound-controlling experiment above (and Limitation 1, Section 8) identifies a second, distinct causal pathway — turnover instability driven by the pretrained embedding-based state representation itself, rather than by transaction costs — which in principle can arise regardless of rebalancing cadence. An earlier draft cited KOSPI's own single-policy failure (Section 1.1) as an instance of this architecture-driven pathway, but the rerun above showed that failure to be a pipeline artifact, so the KOSPI test below should be read without assuming the pathway is present on KOSPI. Read this way, the test below asks whether the same remedy that fixed architecture-driven instability on crypto — specialist diversity plus inference-time smoothing — also transfers to the same instability at a different cadence, not whether EarnHFT's high-frequency-specific mechanism itself generalizes to slow markets.

**KOSPI: partially closing this gap with the final recipe.** Reimplementing the full Section 4 cascade — including the top-$K$-plus-gate structural fix and the router experiments — on KOSPI remains a substantial undertaking, comparable in scope to repeating most of Section 4 on a second dataset, and we still do not attempt it. Instead we run a smaller test: porting only the already-validated final recipe — a Q-teacher-free $\beta$-conditioned specialist pool (Stage A/B, priority sampling, no router) plus inference-time exponential smoothing — to KOSPI's 5-cluster portfolio environment. We omit the top-$K$-plus-gate mechanism (Section 4.4), which is a structural response to dense-softmax collapse specific to the 44-asset setting, and the router, which was ultimately confirmed to contribute zero value in crypto (Sections 4.5–4.6). We exclude 2023 entirely from training to obtain a genuinely unseen validation window, and hold the 2024–2025 test period identical to the single-policy experiment of Section 1.1. This HRL test predates the rerun above and still uses the original (defective) pipeline's embeddings and cluster assignments; it was re-evaluated under the corrected environment of Section 3.3 but not retrained on corrected embeddings.

The result reproduces only partially and is, on balance, negative. The diversity diagnostic differs from v3's outright collapse in Section 4.3: of the four specialists, $\beta=30$ is clearly distinguished from the others (weight-trajectory correlation 0.22–0.51), but the extreme $\beta$ pair ($\beta=\pm90$) converges to essentially the same policy (correlation 0.955) — neither full collapse nor full diversity (with the caveat that on KOSPI's 2021–2022 daily training period Stage A produced only 7 regime chunks, so $\beta$-conditioned sampling repeatedly drew from a handful of segments). The direction of inertial smoothing also differs: only $\beta=-30$ favors low $\alpha$ (strong inertia) in validation as in crypto, while the other three specialists favor high $\alpha$ (weak inertia) instead — plausibly because KOSPI's daily rebalancing cadence, unlike crypto's 30-minute cycle, has little high-frequency noise for inertia to absorb in the first place. Critically, the candidate selected purely on validation Sharpe ($\beta=90$, $\alpha=0.3$) beats equal-weight in validation (Sharpe 1.477 vs. 1.302) but fails outright in test (Sharpe 0.137 vs. 1.038) — applying the same validation/test consistency criterion from Section 4.6 to KOSPI rejects this candidate, in the same form as (though the opposite direction of) the crypto $\beta=30$ case: strong in one period, weak in the other.

We read this as a result with two implications. First, it shows the validation/test consistency diagnostic working as intended on a new market — the same procedure developed on crypto caught, on KOSPI, a candidate that headline validation-period numbers alone would have gotten deployed. Second, crypto's specific mechanisms (the exact shape of the diversity collapse, the benefit of inertial smoothing) do not transfer as-is — equal-weight and the corrected single-policy PPO (ten-run mean Sharpe about 1.08) both beat this KOSPI HRL candidate by a wide margin. Because that single policy's edge is reproduced by a static cluster tilt, no RL configuration we have tested beats equal-weight on KOSPI through period-by-period decisions. And because this HRL candidate was trained on the original defective embeddings, part of its failure may be due to those defects. Because this test omits Section 4.4's structural intervention, it does not let us conclude that the full Section 4 cascade fails to generalize to KOSPI — only that (a) the degree of Q-teacher-free diversity collapse may depend on asset count and action-space dimensionality, and (b) the validation/test consistency diagnostic itself appears to be a market-agnostic, robust methodological tool. Porting the full structural intervention of Section 4.4 to KOSPI remains future work. Read against the narrower framing established above, this negative result means the remedy for *architecture-driven* turnover instability does not transfer to KOSPI either — it is not evidence that EarnHFT's high-frequency-specific mechanism fails in slow markets, a claim this experiment was never positioned to test in the first place.

**U.S. large-cap equities: the same final recipe, a third result.** On top of the same raw price-window environment used for the motivating single-policy check above, we port the same reduced recipe used for KOSPI (Q-teacher-free $\beta$-conditioned specialist pool plus inference-time smoothing, no top-$K$-plus-gate, no router) — reused unchanged specifically so the KOSPI and U.S. equity secondary-market checks remain directly comparable to each other, not because the top-$K$-plus-gate mechanism is structurally inapplicable here the way it is to KOSPI's 5-dimensional action space (U.S. equities shares crypto's 44-asset action space, so that specific argument does not apply); porting the full v4 structural fix to U.S. equities specifically remains untested (Section 8, Limitation 1). The diversity diagnostic shows average pairwise weight-trajectory correlation of 0.86–0.98 across the four specialists (top-10 holdings Jaccard overlap 0.61–0.72) — much closer to v3's outright collapse (Section 4.3) than to KOSPI's partial diversity. Inertial smoothing shows yet another pattern: **all four** $\beta$ values favor $\alpha=1.0$ (no smoothing at all) in validation — unlike crypto ("inertia always wins") or KOSPI (mixed), plausibly because a daily rebalancing cadence has little of the high-frequency cost that inertia absorbs on crypto's 30-minute cycle (a hypothesis, untested). The candidate selected on validation Sharpe ($\beta=-90$, $\alpha=1.0$) edges out equal-weight in validation (Sharpe 1.539 vs. 1.506) but is **statistically indistinguishable** from equal-weight (1.454) and single-policy PPO (1.482) in test — 1.465, even marginally below the single policy. Critically, the validation/test Sharpe gap itself is small (0.074) — by that diagnostic alone, this candidate reads as "consistent." But it is consistent at being *no better than* equal-weight, not consistent at *beating* it.

This last point is the mirror image of the KOSPI case: the KOSPI HRL candidate was rejected by a large validation/test gap, while the U.S. equity HRL candidate passes the gap criterion but delivers a result with nothing to be consistent *about*. Both failures show that Section 4.6's success criterion is a conjunction, not a single number — **both** a small validation/test gap **and** positive performance are required. The $\beta=90$ specialist in Section 4.6 (gap 0.109, the smallest in the crypto experiments, but negative in both periods) makes the identical point independently: a small gap alone does not certify a policy's quality.

**Synthesizing three markets.**

| Market | State representation | Single policy vs. equal-weight (§1.1) | Final HRL recipe vs. equal-weight | Val/test gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Crypto (deployed) | Embeddings | Fails badly (Sharpe −4.134 vs. 0.255) | **Ahead (+6.66% vs. −0.81%, Sharpe 1.310 vs. 0.255)** — positive in both periods only for the deployed seed, Section 6.1 | Small (0.396) |

The fourth row is the crux of this table: holding the market fixed at U.S. large-cap and switching only the state representation, from raw price windows to embeddings, moved performance from "tied" to "clearly behind" — directly confirming, within this project's own data, that architecture is a cause of the failure. But the size of that drop (1.482 → 1.251) falls well short of the collapse seen on crypto (−4.134), which also implicates market or regime as a separate modulator of severity. The three markets otherwise exhibit three distinct outcomes: crypto is the only success this paper tests (small gap plus genuine outperformance — though limited to the deployed seed, Section 6.1); KOSPI is a rejection caught by the gap diagnostic; U.S. large-cap is a small gap with nothing behind it. In two of the three markets, the final recipe fails to beat equal-weight — this paper has no basis for claiming its deployed policy generalizes across markets, and does not make that claim. What we do report is that the same diagnostic procedure established on crypto (validation/test consistency), applied unchanged to two different markets, produced defensible — if negative — verdicts in both cases, and that the single-policy failure motivating Section 1.1 is caused by architecture and modulated in severity by market or regime. That is the full extent of what this paper can claim about the portability of its methodology and failure mechanisms, as distinct from the portability of the deployed policy itself.

**Same asset class, different timescale: a point-in-time 13-asset multi-year walk-forward.** The three-market comparison above asked how far the final recipe transfers when the *market* changes. Limitation 5 (Section 8) wanted to ask the same question within crypto itself, along the *time* axis, but concluded that genuine multi-year walk-forward was structurally impossible on the 44-asset universe because of listing age. This subsection reports a follow-up that partially overturns that conclusion.

*Constructing a point-in-time universe.* Rather than re-selecting the universe by today's trading volume, we consulted CoinMarketCap's historical snapshot for 2021-01-01 (top-20 by market capitalization) and, after excluding stablecoins (USDT, USDC) and a wrapped asset (WBTC), arrived at 17 "real" assets. Of these, 13 (BTC, ETH, XRP, LTC, BCH, ADA, BNB, LINK, XLM, TRX, DOT, THETA, XTZ) were confirmed still active on Binance via the exchange's API at verification time; 4 (BSV, EOS, XMR, XEM) were confirmed inactive or delisted — these four are the residual survivorship bias this experiment cannot resolve (point-in-time selection removes look-ahead bias, but the structural limitation that delisted-coin history is unavailable from free sources remains). Ranks 21–50 could not be verified due to scraping limitations, so this universe is strictly scoped to "top-20 by January 2021 market cap that is still tradable today" — it does not claim the same breadth as the original 44-asset track.

*Split and method.* Train 2021–2023 (3 years) / validation 2024 (1 year, genuinely unseen) / test 2025–2026 (through the end of available data, roughly 1.5 years) — the same split structure as the KOSPI track. We port the already-validated final recipe unchanged: a Q-teacher-free $\beta$-conditioned specialist pool (Stage A/B, no router) plus inference-time smoothing, omitting top-$K$-plus-gate since its structural motivation is weak at this small a scale.

*First result — an extreme regime reversal.* The validation period (2024) was an extraordinary bull run in which equal-weight alone returned +90.7% (Sharpe 1.307). Sweeping all 28 $\beta \times \alpha$ combinations on this period selected $\beta=-30$, $\alpha=0.01$ (validation Sharpe 1.449, narrowly ahead of equal-weight), which we then ran once on the test period (2025–2026, in which equal-weight fell −61.0%). The result: net return −52.10% (Sharpe −0.417) — better than equal-weight (−60.98%, Sharpe −0.497) and with a smaller drawdown (−64.94% vs. −68.57%, though the margin is only 3.6 points, far short of the 44-asset experiment's 82% relative reduction) — but a validation-to-test Sharpe gap of 1.866, far beyond the deployed policy's 0.396 and close to momentum's 2.490. Running all four $\beta$ candidates standalone shows the same pattern without exception (gaps of 1.759–1.952).

*Second check — confirming the reversal is not a fluke of one split.* To test whether this failure is an artifact of the specific "2024 bull into 2025 crash" pairing, we reused the same four frozen $\beta$ policies (trained once on 2021–2023, no retraining) and repeated the identical procedure (select on validation, evaluate once on test) across four adjacent six-month blocks spanning 2024–2026. Three of four folds exceeded a 0.3 gap (0.784 / 2.526 / 0.220 / 2.184, mean 1.429). The one fold under 0.3 (2025 H1 → H2, 0.220) had validation and test Sharpes of only 0.01 and 0.23, offering little to be consistent about in the first place — a "small gap, no performance" case like the $\beta=90$ specialist of Section 4.6. None of five independent (validation, test) observations combined a small gap with positive performance.

*Third check — ruling out diversity collapse as the cause.* Given the v3 precedent (Section 4.3), we first had to rule out that this failure was, once again, "diversity never emerged." Pairwise weight-trajectory correlation among the four $\beta$ experts over the test period averaged 0.380 (range 0.302 to 0.565) — far from v3's near-total collapse ($\approx$1) and about as well separated as the KOSPI reduced recipe (0.22–0.51). (Before the accounting correction this averaged −0.153; the corrected environment records post-return holdings, so all four experts' weights move together with the market, which mechanically raises the correlation.) Top holdings differ meaningfully by $\beta$ ($\beta=-90$ favors ADA/DOT/ETH; $\beta=-30$ favors BCH/BNB/BTC; and so on). **Diversity was genuine here, and every candidate still failed** — a more fundamental failure than v3's, since it rules out the easier fix ("restore diversity") that worked for the 44-asset track.

*Interpretation.* The validation/test consistency diagnostic again showed that no candidate was consistent in any of the five independent observations — it worked as a warning not to trust the candidate the procedure selected. Read together with the 44-asset seed results in Section 6.1 (only 3 of 16 positive in validation), the consistency of a candidate selected on a single validation window is likely specific to that window and seed. But this result is not direct evidence that the 44-asset, 18-month deployed policy collapses at the same scale (it is a different model, trained on a different universe and different data) — what it does mean is that this paper's own evidence, now that the time axis has actually been extended, argues against treating "a small validation/test gap" as proof of genuine multi-year robustness. This is why the 44-asset experiment's downside-protection profile (Section 1.4, Contribution 4) should not be read as evidence of robustness validated over a long horizon.

## 8. Limitations

We list the limitations of this study directly, in the order they arise across Sections 3–7, rather than only in aggregate:

1. **The core structural interventions of the detailed cascade (Section 4) remain validated on one market, and the final recipe beat equal-weight on only one of three markets tested.** As discussed in Section 7, the structural fix (Section 4.4) and router collapse (Section 4.5) are interventions specific to the 44-asset dense action space, not ported to KOSPI or U.S. equities, and remain crypto-specific findings. A related framing caveat, introduced in Section 3.1 and addressed directly in Section 7: both tracks in this paper run several orders of magnitude slower than EarnHFT's original second-level setting, and KOSPI's daily cadence in particular lacks the high-frequency transaction-cost mechanism EarnHFT's hierarchical structure was designed to address in the first place — so the KOSPI results below should be read as testing whether a remedy for a *different*, frequency-agnostic failure mode (architecture-driven turnover instability, discussed later in this same item) transfers across markets, not as a literal generalization test of EarnHFT's high-frequency mechanism itself. This caveat does not apply in the same way to the crypto track, whose 30-minute rebalancing cadence — while still far slower than EarnHFT's original setting — does retain a real, demonstrated transaction-cost-drag mechanism (Section 4.1). The reason differs across the two markets: KOSPI's 5-dimensional action space makes the top-$K$-plus-gate mechanism structurally inapplicable, but U.S. equities shares crypto's 44-asset action space, so that argument does not extend there — the reduced recipe was used instead to keep the two secondary-market checks directly comparable to each other, and porting the full v4 structural fix to U.S. equities specifically has simply not been done, left for future work. The two reduced-scope final-recipe experiments reported in Section 7 (KOSPI, U.S. large-cap) provide solid evidence for the validation/test consistency diagnostic itself (it reached a defensible verdict in both cases), but the deployed recipe failed to beat equal-weight in both markets — by a large validation/test gap on KOSPI, and by simply having no edge to be consistent about on U.S. equities despite a small gap there. The claim that crypto's specific mechanisms (the exact shape of diversity collapse, the benefit of inertial smoothing, the final performance) generalize across markets is therefore not supported by this data — only the diagnostic procedure's portability is. The initial U.S. equity check also varied the state representation along with the market (Section 1.1, Section 7 — raw price windows instead of embeddings, a deliberate choice made for tractability), so a follow-up held the market fixed and equipped the same U.S. equity data with Chronos zero-shot embeddings instead (Section 7). Performance dropped from tied-with-equal-weight to clearly behind it, consistent with architecture as a genuine causal factor — but the drop was far smaller than the collapse observed on crypto, indicating market or regime independently modulates the severity of the failure. This resolves the confound only partially: we now have evidence that architecture matters, not just that it is correlated with market in this dataset, but we cannot yet quantify how much of the *crypto-scale* collapse is attributable to architecture versus regime-specific factors (e.g., asset correlation structure, trend strength) that a single follow-up cannot isolate. The confound-controlling comparison itself is also a single training run per arm, unlike the 16-seed validation behind the deployed policy (Section 6.1) — the controlled design (only the state representation varied, everything else held fixed) supports a causal reading, but run-to-run noise has not been ruled out as a partial explanation for the observed gap. A fully resolved account would require repeating this embedding-vs-raw-window comparison across multiple seeds and on additional markets or regimes, left for future work. Separately, the $\lambda$-sweep in Section 4.3 — a controlled experiment manipulating objective-level signal strength as a single variable and observing a monotonic response in the predicted direction — supports the reading that v3's diversity collapse is controllable via the diagnosed mechanism rather than an artifact of incidental implementation-specific tuning, but this too is confirmed only within the v3 architecture family; empirically repeating this argument on hierarchical RL families other than EarnHFT (e.g., Option-Critic) remains undone.
2. **PPO/DQN hyperparameters remain fixed without a sweep, and the post-hoc top-K sweep is only partial.** The PPO/DQN hyperparameters in Section 3.3 were chosen once, empirically, and held fixed across all of v1–v4 specifically to keep the cross-configuration comparisons in Section 5 uncontaminated by unequal tuning effort — but this means we cannot rule out that different PPO settings would materially change any single configuration's result. $K=10$ in the top-$K$ selection (Section 4.4) was later swept post hoc over neighboring values $\{5,7,15\}$ (Section 6.8), confirming that $K=10$ is the only swept value to pass the validation/test consistency criterion (pre-correction environment, not recomputed) — though the actual swept range ($\{5,7,10,15\}$) does not exactly match the range this paragraph originally flagged as a future candidate ($\{5,10,15,20\}$), and wider values (notably $K=20$) remain untested. The same sweep also revealed that the confidence logits used for top-$K$ selection carry near-zero rank correlation (Spearman IC) with realized returns (Section 6.8) — differences in performance across $K$ should not be over-interpreted as evidence of accurate stock selection.
3. **DQN router training is not deterministic even at fixed seed, and this paper substitutes standard DQN for the original paper's DDQN.** Repeated training runs of the router (Section 4.1, 4.5) with identical seeds and hyperparameters have been observed to vary by roughly ±8 percentage points in net return, attributed to GPU-level non-determinism in the training pipeline. This means single-run comparisons involving a freshly trained router — including the original-vs-matched router comparison in Section 4.5, prior to the decisive ablation that showed both had collapsed to the same constant policy — carry irreducible noise on top of whatever architectural difference is being measured, and any future router-based configuration should be evaluated over repeated training runs before its result is trusted in isolation. Separately, the original EarnHFT paper uses DDQN for both Stage I and Stage III (Section 2.4), while this paper uses standard DQN for implementation simplicity — DDQN was designed specifically to correct standard DQN's overestimation bias, so the early convergence to a single fixed action observed in the router (Section 4.5) cannot be fully attributed to the diversity-signal-absence diagnosis proposed here rather than, partly or wholly, to standard DQN's known overestimation bias. We tested this directly: retraining the matched router with everything else held fixed and only DQN swapped for DDQN, the router still converged 100% to $\beta=30$ on both splits (92/92 chunks on validation, 195/195 on test), and net return and Sharpe ratio matched the original DQN-based matched router to two decimal places (validation $-0.19\%$/Sharpe $0.025$, test $+7.48\%$/Sharpe $3.046$, pre-correction environment; this DDQN experiment was not recomputed). Reproducing the identical collapse under an algorithm specifically designed to correct overestimation bias suggests that standard DQN's overestimation bias was not the cause — the observed router collapse appears attributable, independent of algorithm choice, to the mechanism diagnosed in Section 4.5 (matching the episode\_progress training/serving distributions removed the router's diversity signal entirely).
4. **The front-loaded-returns question in Section 4.4 is not fully resolved.** The v4 policy pool's test-period return is somewhat front-loaded (+5.55% in the first half of the window vs. +3.62% in the second), and while turnover and asset-rotation diagnostics rule out a pure static-position artifact, a full explanation of the front/back-half asymmetry (e.g., a regime-decomposition analysis) has not been carried out.
5. **Multi-year walk-forward validation was not possible on the original 44-asset universe — but we ran it on a point-in-time reduced universe, and it failed.** Only 23 of the 44 assets have price history predating 2022, and none predate 2021, so a genuine multi-year walk-forward evaluation (as opposed to the single-year validation/test split used throughout Sections 4–6) is not achievable on the full universe without first shrinking it to a survivorship-biased subset. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Section 6.2) is used as a partial substitute, but it corrects for a different bias (Section 6.2) than a multi-year walk-forward would, and does not fully close this gap. As reported in Section 7, we constructed a point-in-time 13-asset universe by cross-referencing CoinMarketCap's January 2021 market-cap snapshot against live exchange listings, and ran a genuine multi-year walk-forward (train 2021–2023, validate 2024, test 2025–2026) with the same methodology behind the deployed policy ($\beta$-conditioned specialist pool plus validation-selected smoothing). The result: across five independent (validation, test) observations — one formal split plus four no-retraining rolling-refit folds — no observation combined a small gap with positive performance (validation/test Sharpe gaps 0.22–2.53, mean 1.43; the one fold under 0.3 had Sharpe of only 0.01–0.23 in both periods). A separate diagnostic ruled out diversity collapse (Section 4.3's cause for v3) as the explanation — the four $\beta$ experts' weight-trajectory correlations averaged 0.380, well separated unlike v3's collapse. This is not direct evidence that the 44-asset, 18-month deployed policy collapses at the same scale (it is a different model, on a different universe, trained on different data) — but it means this paper's central diagnostic criterion, "a small validation/test gap," is not supported by this data as a guarantee of genuine multi-year robustness. The same conclusion arises within the 44-asset experiment: training the deployed configuration with other seeds gives negative validation Sharpe for 13 of 16 (Section 6.1).
6. **No configuration in this study reaches conventional statistical significance under DSR.** Both the deployed seed and the raw-maximum seed have DSR below the conventional 0.95 threshold at both $N=6$ and $N=16$ (Section 6.2). The deployment argument in this paper rests on validation/test consistency (Section 4.6, 6.1), not on achieving formal statistical significance — readers should not conflate "the most robust configuration we tested" with "a result proven significant by a multiple-comparisons-corrected test."
7. **The transaction-cost model is a fixed proportional fee with no slippage or market-impact term.** $\tau=0.001$ matches Binance's standard taker fee at the account's current scale, but does not model slippage or the price impact of larger orders; this is adequate at the live account's current size (Section 9) but would need revisiting before drawing conclusions at meaningfully larger capital. A post-hoc sensitivity sweep over $\tau \in \{0.05\%, \ldots, 0.2\%\}$ (0.5–2x) shows this assumption is not load-bearing for the conclusion — even at 2x cost, the deployed policy's test Sharpe (1.113) remains more than 4x the equal-weight benchmark (0.255) — though this sweep still leaves the fixed-proportional-fee model itself, and the slippage/market-impact gap, unaddressed.
8. **The EIIE and LSRE-CAAN baselines (Section 6.6) reimplement only the network topology, not the original training procedure.** Both original papers' training methods (EIIE's OSBL, LSRE-CAAN's direct policy gradient) are replaced with this paper's PPO pipeline; the comparisons in Section 6.6 should be read as "each architecture under our training regime," not as full reproductions of Jiang, Xu, & Liang (2017) or Li, Zhang, Yang, & Chen (2023).
9. **This is an empirical case study and diagnostic methodology, not a new algorithm.** Every architectural component in Sections 4 and the diagnostics in Section 6 are adaptations of existing published methods (Section 2); the paper's contribution is the documented failure cascade and the validation methodology built to interrogate it, which is why we position this work for an applied-science venue (Section 1) rather than a methods-focused main research track.
10. **Live deployment evidence is preliminary.** The final policy (Section 4.6) is running on a real exchange account as of this writing, but the live trading log has been accumulating only since 2026-07-29 and is not yet long enough to serve as an independent validation of the backtested results reported in Sections 5–6; we report the live deployment as the current operational status of this work, not as additional evidence for its claims.
11. **The post-correction recomputation re-ran evaluation only, and some experiments remain at pre-correction values.** After fixing the holding-drift and untradable-asset handling of Section 3.3 we recomputed the crypto performance figures of Sections 4–6, but reused the policies and routers trained under the pre-correction environment (whose reward omitted rebalancing costs). Retraining under the corrected reward could change the policies themselves. The objective-injection experiment (Section 4.3), the top-$K$ sweep (Section 6.8), and the DDQN experiment (Limitation 3) remain pre-correction values. The KOSPI, U.S. large-cap, and point-in-time experiments of Section 7 were recomputed under the corrected environment (only the KOSPI single-policy PPO was retrained; the rest were re-evaluated), but the Section 7 KOSPI HRL pool was trained on the original, defective embedding pipeline.

## 9. Conclusion

This paper traced what happens when a hierarchical reinforcement learning architecture designed for single-asset, discrete-position high-frequency trading (EarnHFT) is adapted to continuous, multi-asset portfolio allocation, across independently built market datasets. The starting motivation — that single-policy RL using a pretrained, embedding-based state fails to beat equal-weighting — held up on cryptocurrency. The Korean-equity result that first looked like the same failure was overturned by a rerun with pipeline defects fixed (the corrected single policy beat equal-weight, but its edge is reproduced by a static cluster tilt), and the failure did not reproduce on U.S. large-cap equities initially checked with a raw price-window state representation either. A follow-up that held the market fixed and re-equipped the same U.S. equity data with an embedding-based state (Section 7) reversed this, moving performance from "tied" back to "clearly behind" — consistent with architecture as a genuine causal factor in the failure, though not as catastrophic as on crypto, implicating market or regime as a separate modulator of severity (Section 7, Limitation 1). The subsequent architectural investigation, conducted on the cryptocurrency track, surfaced a sequence of failures that would not have been visible from headline test-period metrics alone: a cost-driven signal loss fixable only at inference time, not in training; a diversity collapse traceable to the absence of a dynamic-programming teacher signal; a structural fix that restored diversity but left the resulting policy's routing layer to quietly degenerate into a constant function; and a specific specialist agent whose apparent test-period superiority turned out, on a targeted validation/test comparison, to be overfitting rather than skill. The policy that survived this process — a single specialist agent combined with a fixed-rate inference-time smoothing filter, with no router at all — is also the simplest configuration considered at any point in the study, and, despite not achieving the highest raw return, it is the only one of the ten strategies in Table 2 with positive Sharpe in both the validation and test periods. However, most other seeds of the same configuration lose money in validation, as does a clean retrain that excluded that window from training — positive performance in both periods is a property of the deployed seed, not of the configuration.

We take two lessons from this arc to be of use beyond this specific project. First, when adapting a method across a significant change in problem structure — here, from single-asset discrete actions to multi-asset continuous ones — it is worth explicitly identifying which components of the original method are load-bearing for its key mechanism (diversity, in EarnHFT's case) before assuming a partial port will preserve that mechanism; Section 4.3 and its corroborating single-asset check (Section 6.7) show this can be diagnosed directly rather than discovered by accident. Second, and more broadly, a policy's headline test-period performance is not sufficient evidence of its quality on its own — validation-period performance and the two-period Sharpe gap exposed a router collapse (Section 4.5) and a specialist that is good only in the test window (Section 4.6), which Sharpe ratio and maximum drawdown evaluated on the test period alone did not. But applying the same diagnostic across the seed distribution shows that the deployed policy's own consistency rests on a single seed (Section 6.1): a result selected on one validation window and one seed should be reported together with its seed-level distribution, even when it passes that diagnostic. We report this diagnostic, and its relationship to the (distinct, complementary) Deflated Sharpe Ratio, as a methodological contribution independent of the specific trading results.

The policy described in Section 4.6 is currently deployed on a live exchange account. The transaction-cost sensitivity sweep (Section 8, limitation 7), two reduced-scope generalization tests on KOSPI and U.S. large-cap equities (Section 7), and a follow-up that directly controlled the market/architecture confound (Section 7 — confirming architecture as a causal factor, with market/regime left as a likely modulator of severity) are now complete — the final recipe beat equal-weight on only one of the three markets tested, but the validation/test consistency diagnostic reached a defensible verdict on the new markets, and the causal structure behind Section 1.1's motivating failure is now better understood. Extending the evidence base with a longer live-trading record, reimplementing the full Section 4 cascade — including the structural intervention of Section 4.4 — on the KOSPI universe, and repeating the embedding-vs-raw-window comparison across additional markets or regimes to quantify architecture's contribution relative to market/regime, are the most direct remaining next steps, and are left for future work given this paper's present scope and timeline. A separate line of work — proposing and validating a new algorithmic mechanism for restoring specialist diversity without a dynamic-programming teacher, rather than documenting how its absence manifests — would be needed to meet the novelty bar of a methods-focused main research track; we regard the present paper, targeted at an applied-science venue, as a foundation for that follow-on work rather than a substitute for it.

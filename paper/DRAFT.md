# Diagnosing Overfitting in Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning for Portfolio Allocation: A Failure-Mode Cascade and Consistency-First Robustness Methodology Across Three Markets

*Target venue: KDD Applied Data Science track / ACM ICAIF (2027 cycle). Draft status: Abstract + Introduction, first pass.*

---

## Abstract

Hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL) methods such as EarnHFT have shown strong results for single-asset, discrete-position high-frequency trading, but their behavior when ported to continuous, multi-asset portfolio allocation is largely undocumented. This paper is an empirical case study and diagnostic methodology rather than a new algorithm: we catalog the failure modes that emerge when porting EarnHFT-style hierarchical RL to portfolio allocation, and formalize a validation/test consistency diagnostic that catches overfitting invisible to headline test-period metrics alone. We report an extended case study — spanning a Korean equity market (KOSPI, 781 stocks) and a cryptocurrency market (44 USDT pairs on Binance) — in which a naive single-policy PPO agent fails to beat an equal-weight benchmark in both markets (+0.28% vs. +34.33% on KOSPI; −75.68% vs. +2.66% on crypto), motivating a hierarchical redesign. Directly porting EarnHFT's three-stage architecture without its Q-teacher pretraining stage produces a policy pool whose real, positive pre-cost signal (+12.6%) is entirely erased by 30-minute-cadence rebalancing costs (net −32.24%); an inference-time inertia filter recovers this signal (+11.05% net) with no retraining, but the seemingly more principled fix of baking the same inertia into training instead destroys the underlying alpha (pre-cost alpha collapses from +12.6% to +0.92%) via a broken credit-assignment signal. A more faithful reimplementation of EarnHFT's selection procedure — still without a Q-teacher — surfaces a second, independent failure: the specialist pool collapses to near-uniform weights (no diversity engine). Restoring diversity by structurally sparsifying the action space (top-K selection with a risk gate, adapting FreQuant and DeepClair) recovers a diverse policy pool, but the router placed on top of it is later shown, via a decisive ablation, to have collapsed into a constant function equivalent to a single specialist agent — meaning the entire routing stage contributed zero value in this setting. A systematic comparison of all specialist agents across a held-out validation period and the test period reveals that the specialist favored by the router owed its apparent test-set superiority to overfitting, not to the router's discernment. We formalize the diagnostic that exposed this — the gap between validation-period and test-period Sharpe ratios, further confirmed with a block permutation test — and validate the final deployed policy (a single specialist agent with inference-time exponential smoothing, no router) against 16 independent random seeds, a Deflated Sharpe Ratio correction, a leakage-free clean-validation re-run, an ensemble baseline, sensitivity sweeps over the top-K selection width and transaction costs, and both classical (buy-and-hold, cross-sectional momentum, mean-variance optimization, risk parity) and deep-learning (EIIE, LSRE-CAAN) portfolio baselines. Under a consistency-first success criterion — positive, stable performance in both validation and test, rather than peak test-period return — the deployed policy is the only one of ten tested configurations that succeeds: classical momentum achieves a 20x higher raw test return, but also the least trustworthy validation/test Sharpe gap of any strategy tested (2.742, vs. 0.158 for the deployed policy), consistent with a test-period-specific artifact rather than a verifiable edge. We argue that the resulting catalog of failure modes and the accompanying robustness-diagnostic methodology are of independent value to practitioners adapting single-asset HRL trading methods to portfolio settings.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation: single-policy RL with embedding-based state repeatedly fails at portfolio allocation

Reinforcement learning has been proposed repeatedly as a way to learn portfolio allocation policies directly from data, sidestepping the need for hand-specified return and risk models. In practice, however, a plain single-policy agent trained end-to-end on a portfolio's asset universe is a fragile baseline. We observed this failure independently in two structurally unrelated markets. On a Korean equity universe of 781 stocks, a PPO agent operating over a 325-dimensional state (five cluster embeddings plus current weights) and a 5-dimensional cluster-weight action space returned +0.28% over a 2024–2025 test window, against an equal-weight benchmark of +34.33% (Sharpe 0.100 vs. 1.043). On a cryptocurrency universe of 44 USDT spot pairs on Binance, the same architectural pattern — a single PPO policy over asset-level embeddings and softmax portfolio weights — returned −75.68% over a 2026 H1 test window (Sharpe −4.209), against an equal-weight benchmark of +2.66%. The two experiments differ in asset class, state design, action dimensionality, and training period, yet fail in the same direction and by a wide margin. This cross-market consistency is what motivates a hierarchical redesign rather than further tuning of the single-policy baseline: the failure appears to be structural to the single-policy formulation, not an artifact of one dataset.

Both observations share a pretrained, embedding-based state representation and a dense softmax action space over the full asset universe. A third, independent check reported in Section 7 — 44 large-cap U.S. equities, first using a raw price-window state with no embeddings — did not reproduce this failure: single-policy PPO finished the 2024–2025 test window essentially tied with the equal-weight benchmark (+46.20%/Sharpe 1.498 vs. +45.74%/Sharpe 1.473). Because this check varies the state representation along with the market, it could not be read as isolating the market variable alone — so we ran a follow-up on the same U.S. equity market and the same test window, this time with Chronos zero-shot embeddings (the same family of state representation used for Korean equities and crypto), holding the market fixed and controlling for architecture (Section 7). Result: single-policy performance moved from "tied" to "clearly behind" equal-weight (test Sharpe 1.498 → 1.252, against equal-weight's 1.477), accompanied by a 24x jump in average turnover (0.38% to 9.2–9.4% per step) — the same cost-driven mechanism documented for v1 below ("turnover erodes alpha"). That is, **architecture (an embedding-based dense action space) is a genuine causal factor** in the failure, though not as catastrophic here as on crypto (Sharpe −4.209) or Korean equities (0.100 vs. 1.043) — market or regime appears to modulate the severity of the failure. This paper takes the failure observed, consistent in direction though varying in magnitude, across three markets using embedding-based architectures (Korean equities, crypto, U.S. equities; Section 7) as the motivation for a hierarchical redesign.

### 1.2 A failure-mode cascade when porting EarnHFT to continuous portfolios

Rather than leave the cause of single-policy failure as an ungrounded hypothesis, we find a direct precedent in EarnHFT's own reported results. EarnHFT compares against exactly this kind of single-policy baseline (PPO, DQN) in its own (single-asset, high-frequency) setting, and these baselines underperform EarnHFT's hierarchical architecture across all four of its crypto datasets (BTCUSDT, BTCTUSDT, ETHUSDT, GALAUSDT) — EarnHFT leads on total return in all four and on Sharpe ratio in three of four, with PPO and DQN frequently producing negative returns (e.g., on ETHUSDT: PPO −22.61%, DQN −11.02%, vs. EarnHFT +4.52%; their Table 2). EarnHFT's authors attribute this to single policy-based agents converging to a "dummy policy" and single value-based agents lacking robustness across market-trend shifts (Qin et al., 2024) — precisely the kind of failure we independently observe in Section 1.1 (a single PPO policy trailing equal-weight by a wide margin in both KOSPI and crypto). This precedent — not an abstract expectation from hierarchical RL theory in general, but EarnHFT's own demonstration that this exact failure pattern is addressed by hierarchical decomposition in its own setting — is why this paper selects EarnHFT's three-stage structure as the architecture to port.

EarnHFT (Qin et al., 2024) addresses a related but narrower problem — high-frequency, single-asset, discrete-position trading — with a three-stage hierarchy: a dynamic-programming "Q-teacher" (Stage I) that pretrains a large pool of agents biased toward distinct risk preferences, a distillation stage that retains a small diverse subset (Stage II), and a router that switches between them based on market regime (Stage III). We report what happens when this architecture is ported to a setting EarnHFT was not designed for — continuous portfolio weights over 44 simultaneously-held assets — and, critically, without Stage I, which presupposes a discrete, single-asset action space and is not straightforward to generalize.

The resulting investigation surfaces a cascade of failures, each with its own diagnosis and attempted fix, which forms the empirical core of this paper:

- **Direct port (v1).** Reproducing Stages II–III without a Q-teacher (using only priority-weighted sampling in place of Stage I) yields a policy pool and router that lose −32.24% net of trading costs. Decomposing the result shows the underlying signal is strongly positive (+12.6% *before* costs, already beating the benchmark) but is erased by transaction costs from 30-minute rebalancing at 5.4% average per-step turnover.
- **Inference-time fix works; training-time fix destroys the signal.** Applying an exponential inertia filter at inference time only (`w_new = (1-α)w_old + α·softmax(a)`, α=0.01) recovers +11.05% net of costs (Sharpe 0.638) without any retraining. Attempting the more principled-seeming alternative — baking the same inertia into the training environment so the policy learns low-turnover behavior directly — instead collapses the pre-cost alpha from +12.6% to +0.92% and nets −3.58%. We attribute this to a broken credit-assignment signal: when the environment itself damps the executed action, the policy's actions become only loosely coupled to the reward it receives.
- **A faithful reimplementation still lacks a diversity engine (v3).** Reimplementing EarnHFT more closely — adding a cash action and a strict train/validation split for agent selection, as in the original paper — still fails (+0.68% net, below the equal-weight benchmark), because the pool of specialist agents converges to near-identical, near-uniform policies. We trace this to the absence of Stage I: without a Q-teacher directly injecting distinct behavioral targets, using the risk-preference parameter only to bias data sampling is not sufficient to produce diversity in a continuous, 45-dimensional softmax action space.
- **Restoring diversity via a structural action-space change (v4).** Replacing the dense softmax action with a structurally sparse one — top-K asset selection (adapting FreQuant, Jeon et al., KDD'24) combined with a dedicated risk-exposure gate (adapting DeepClair, Choi et al., CIKM'24) — restores measurable diversity across the specialist pool (turnover remains healthy throughout the test window; top-10 holdings rotate through 33 of 44 assets) and, combined with inference-time smoothing, achieves the best risk-adjusted result in the study **with the router still in place**: +8.26% net of costs, Sharpe 2.168, MDD −5.22% — a number the next two bullets revisit and revise downward, once the router itself is shown to be doing nothing.
- **The router collapses to a constant function.** A subsequent fix targeting a train/serve mismatch in the router's inputs appears, at first, to further improve results (Sharpe 2.168 → 3.046). A decisive follow-up ablation shows this improvement is illusory: running the router's single most-selected specialist agent alone, with no router at all, reproduces the "improved" result to four decimal places. The router had not learned to route — it had degenerated into always selecting one fixed agent.
- **That one agent's apparent superiority is itself overfitting.** Systematically re-evaluating all four candidate specialist agents on both a held-out validation period and the test period shows that the specific agent favored by the collapsed router is the *only* one of the four whose performance swings wildly between the two periods (validation Sharpe 0.025 → test Sharpe 3.046), while the other three — including the one ultimately selected for deployment — remain stable across both periods. This validation/test Sharpe gap, rather than test-period performance alone, is the diagnostic that exposes the overfitting; we argue it deserves to be reported alongside, not instead of, headline test-period numbers in RL trading papers.

### 1.3 Validating the survivor

The final deployed policy — a single specialist agent with inference-time exponential smoothing and no router — is deliberately the simplest configuration to survive this cascade. Rather than treat this simplicity as a stopping point, we subject it to a battery of robustness checks assembled specifically to interrogate the kind of failures uncovered above: reproducibility across 16 independent training seeds (direction of outperformance replicates in all 16; magnitude does not, underscoring that seed-level variance must be reported, not averaged away); a Deflated Sharpe Ratio correction for the multiple-seed selection process (which, counterintuitively, we show does *not* penalize the seed with the highest raw Sharpe more than others — a distinct finding from the validation/test consistency check, since the two diagnostics correct for different biases); a fully leakage-free retraining using a held-out validation window never seen during the original model-selection process (which reproduces, and slightly exceeds, the deployed model's performance, arguing against validation-set leakage as an explanation for the results); an equal-weight ensemble of the 16 seeds (which, unexpectedly, *increases* variance rather than reducing it — a negative result in itself); a post-hoc sensitivity sweep over neighboring top-K selection widths (5, 7, 15), which confirms that the deployed K=10 is the only swept value to pass the validation/test consistency criterion, while separately revealing that the confidence signal used for top-K selection carries near-zero rank correlation with realized returns — a negative result of its own (Section 6.8); a transaction-cost sensitivity sweep from 0.5x to 2x the deployed rate, under which the deployed policy's test Sharpe stays at least 2.6x the equal-weight benchmark's throughout (Section 6.9); a block permutation test that formalizes the validation/test gap diagnostic itself as a hypothesis test, confirming the same direction seen qualitatively though short of conventional significance (Section 6.10); and a comparison against four classical baselines (buy-and-hold, cross-sectional momentum, Ledoit-Wolf minimum-variance optimization, risk parity) and two deep-learning baselines adapted from the literature (EIIE, Jiang et al., 2017; LSRE-CAAN, Li, Zhang, Yang, & Chen, 2023). Under a raw-return criterion, momentum wins by a wide margin (+106.04% vs. +5.66% test return); under a consistency criterion — the same validation/test Sharpe gap diagnostic from Section 1.2 — momentum's result is the least trustworthy of everything tested (gap of 2.742, the largest observed in the entire study), while the deployed policy's gap (0.158) is the smallest among strategies with positive performance in both periods — risk parity's gap is smaller still (0.003), but it is negative in both periods, underscoring that a small gap alone does not certify a policy's quality (Section 6.5). The EIIE and LSRE-CAAN baselines both reproduce, in two entirely different network architectures, the same uniform-weight collapse first observed in v3, reinforcing that the diversity failure is a property of the problem setting rather than of one particular architecture.

### 1.4 Contributions

This paper makes the following contributions:

1. **A causal isolation of why single-policy RL fails.** After observing that embedding-based single-policy RL repeatedly trails equal-weight in two structurally unrelated markets (Korean equities and cryptocurrency), we run a confound-controlling experiment on a third market (U.S. large-cap equities) that holds the market fixed and varies only the state representation (raw price windows vs. pretrained embeddings), directly confirming that this failure is caused by the embedding-based dense action space itself (accompanied by a 24-fold turnover spike) rather than by market-specific chance — with market or regime independently modulating how severe the resulting collapse is (Section 7).
2. **A documented failure-mode cascade** for porting single-asset, discrete-position hierarchical RL (EarnHFT) to continuous, multi-asset portfolios: cost-driven signal erasure, a training-time inertia trap, a Q-teacher-shaped diversity gap, a structural fix via sparse action spaces, and a router that silently degenerates into a constant function.
3. **A robustness-diagnostic methodology** — validation/test Sharpe-gap analysis, shown to be complementary to (not redundant with) the Deflated Sharpe Ratio — exercised across 16 seeds, an ensemble, a leakage-free holdout, and classical and deep-learning baselines, including two supplementary formal significance tests (DSR, a block permutation test) that corroborate its direction without reaching conventional significance.
4. **A simple deployed policy that is empirically robust within the 44-asset, 18-month experiment tested** (one specialist agent, no router, inference-time smoothing) — under a consistency-first success criterion, the only one of ten tested strategies to succeed in both validation and test periods, cutting maximum drawdown to −7.43% against the equal-weight benchmark's −40.09% (an 82% reduction) in this experiment. However, the point-in-time multi-year walk-forward reported in Section 7 shows that the same methodology's validation/test consistency collapses once the time axis is genuinely extended (all five independent (validation, test) observations fail; Section 8, Limitation 5) — so this downside-protection profile should not be read as evidence of capital preservation validated over a long horizon. This policy is currently running as a live trading system on a real exchange account (see Section 8, Limitation 10, for caveats on the live record's current length), but that is a report of continued operation, not additional evidence of robustness.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Hierarchical reinforcement learning for trading

EarnHFT (Qin et al., 2024, AAAI'24; arXiv:2309.12891) is the architectural template this paper builds on and departs from. It targets second-level, single-asset, discrete-position high-frequency trading with a three-stage pipeline: **Stage I** solves a dynamic program offline to obtain an approximately optimal action-value function $Q^*$ for a large number of risk-preference settings $\beta$, and trains a pool of agents via DDQN with a KL-divergence term $\mathrm{KL}(Q_\theta \| Q^*)$ against this teacher signal plus imitation of the optimal actor (their Eq. 2–3), using $\beta$-conditioned priority sampling of training chunks (their Eq. 4); **Stage II** distills this large pool down to a small number of representative agents by evaluating each on a held-out validation split, per market regime; **Stage III** trains a router (in the original paper, a DDQN over market/agent features) that switches between the surviving specialists as the regime changes. The dynamic-programming teacher in Stage I is what allows the pool to be diverse by construction — each agent is trained toward a different, externally supplied optimal policy, rather than discovering diversity on its own.

Hierarchical RL for multi-asset portfolio allocation is, on its own, not unstudied, and this paper does not claim otherwise. HRPM (Wang, Wei, An, Feng, & Yao, 2021, AAAI'21) splits portfolio management into a high-level policy that sets multi-asset weights at low frequency and a low-level policy that executes each rebalance as a sequence of bid/ask orders to minimize slippage — a strategic-allocation/execution split, not the risk-preference-conditioned specialist pool used here. Zha, Dai, Xu, & Wu (2022, IJCNN'22; DOI 10.1109/IJCNN55064.2022.9892378) split the problem the other way: a high-level policy selects a subset of stocks with high profit probability from a large universe, and a low-level policy sets continuous weights only over that selected subset — a selection-then-allocation hierarchy, again without a risk-preference-conditioned pool or a regime router. MetaTrader (Niu, Li, & Li, 2022, CIKM'22; arXiv:2210.01774) pretrains multiple diverse trading policies via a combined RL/imitation objective and then learns a meta-policy that routes among them — structurally the closest of these to EarnHFT's Stage II–III, but pool diversity here is induced by the imitation objective rather than a DP teacher, and the setting is multi-asset from the start rather than ported from a single-asset origin. Millea & Edalat (2023, *International Journal of Financial Studies*, 11(1), 10; DOI 10.3390/ijfs11010010) route, via a DRL agent framed as a stateful multi-armed bandit, among several off-market Hierarchical Risk Parity / Hierarchical Equal Risk Contribution allocators, sidestepping the difficulty of learning continuous weights directly by keeping the low-level allocators non-learned. MacroHFT (Zong, Wang, Qin, Feng, Wang, & An, 2024, KDD'24; arXiv:2406.14537) — a follow-up from an overlapping author set with EarnHFT — extends EarnHFT's own single-asset, high-frequency setting with regime-decomposed sub-agents and a memory-augmented hyper-agent that mixes their outputs, but remains single-asset throughout. Most recently, HARLF (Coriat & Benhamou, 2025; arXiv:2507.18560, a non-peer-reviewed preprint at the time of writing) proposes a three-tier base-agent/meta-agent/super-agent hierarchy for multi-asset allocation augmented with LLM-derived sentiment. None of these six papers, however, attempts to port EarnHFT's specific three-stage pipeline — a DP-teacher-trained specialist pool plus validation-based distillation plus a regime router — onto a continuous multi-asset simplex, and consequently none reports the specific failure sequence this paper documents: signal erosion under realistic transaction costs, diversity collapse in the specialist pool once the DP teacher is removed, and a regime router degenerating into a constant function. That is the gap Section 4 fills, and it is the basis for this paper's narrower claim of contribution (Section 1.4) rather than any claim that hierarchical RL for multi-asset portfolios is itself novel.

This construction is specific to a single, liquid asset with a discretized action space (long / flat / short, in the typical instantiation), where an exact or near-exact DP solution is tractable. It does not directly generalize to a continuous-weight, multi-asset portfolio, where the action space is a $(N{-}1)$-simplex over $N$ assets and no equivalent closed-form optimum is available. Section 4 documents, in detail, what happens when Stages II–III are ported to such a setting while Stage I is replaced with a cheaper substitute (risk-preference-conditioned data sampling only, no teacher signal) — to our knowledge this specific failure mode (diversity collapse without a teacher, and the router's subsequent collapse into a constant function) has not been previously documented for portfolio-scale HRL.

More generally, EarnHFT's three-stage structure is one concrete instance of the options framework (Sutton, Precup, & Singh, 1999) — a fixed set of sub-policies with a higher-level policy switching among them — and belongs to the same "long-horizon manager, short-horizon executor" family as feudal RL (Dayan & Hinton, 1993; extended to deep learning by Vezhnevets et al.'s (2017) FeUdal Networks). These general theories tend to presuppose that sub-policy diversity arises naturally from distinct sub-goals or termination conditions; this paper's failure catalog (Sections 4.3, 4.5) provides a concrete instance of exactly that general form breaking down when the presupposition (played by the Q-teacher in EarnHFT) is absent.

### 2.2 Structured action spaces and policy regularization for portfolio selection

Two recent papers supply the structural ingredients we use to restore specialist diversity once the direct EarnHFT port fails. **FreQuant** (Jeon, Park, Park, & Kang, 2024, KDD'24; DOI 10.1145/3637528.3671668) proposes a frequency-domain RL framework for portfolio optimization built around a Frequency State Encoder that fuses abrupt market events with prevalent multi-frequency patterns; the component we adopt is its confidence-score-based top-$K$ asset selection mechanism (their Eq. 13–14), which we use as a way to sparsify the action space rather than to encode frequency information — the frequency-domain encoder itself is not part of this paper's contribution and is not reimplemented. **DeepClair** (Choi, Kim, Gim, Lee, & Kang, 2024, CIKM'24; arXiv:2407.13427) pretrains a Transformer-based market forecaster and fine-tunes it with LoRA into an RL-based portfolio-selection pipeline; the component we adopt is its dedicated risk-exposure gate that separates *which assets to hold* from *how much total risk to take*, which we implement as a single scalar sigmoid gate $\rho \in (0,1)$ applied on top of the top-$K$ selection. As with FreQuant, DeepClair's forecasting-pretraining stage is not reimplemented — in our pipeline, that role is already filled by the Chronos+MTGNN embedding backbone described in Section 3.2. Both adoptions are therefore partial: we borrow a single structural mechanism from each paper and graft it onto the EarnHFT skeleton, rather than reproducing either paper in full. Section 4.4 describes the resulting environment (`SparseGatedCryptoPortfolioEnv`) precisely. For the same reason, we do not include full reimplementations of FreQuant or DeepClair as baselines in Section 6: a fair comparison would require matching each paper's own frequency-decomposition or forecasting-pretraining budget rather than substituting ours, and doing so for two additional full architectures is a substantially larger undertaking than the topology-only EIIE and LSRE-CAAN baselines we do include (Section 6.6, Limitation 8) — left for future work.

The inertia filter of Section 4.2 relates to a separate literature — action policy smoothing — but as a contrast rather than an adoption. **CAPS** (Mysore, Mabsout, Mancuso, & Saenko, 2021, ICRA'21; arXiv:2012.06644) smooths RL-controller actions for robotic control (e.g., quadrotors) by adding two regularization terms directly to the policy loss: a temporal-smoothness term requiring consecutive actions to resemble one another, and a spatial-smoothness term requiring similar states to map to similar actions, reporting an ~80% reduction in power consumption on a real quadrotor while maintaining flight-worthy control. The second experiment of Section 4.2 (`SmoothStepMixin`, which bakes exponential blending into the training environment itself) is, in fact, an independent rediscovery of the same idea as CAPS's temporal-smoothness term — inducing smoothness directly at training time — applied to this paper's setting. Where CAPS reports success in robotic control, the identical approach here destroys the cost-free alpha via credit-assignment failure (12.6%→0.92%). The solution we ultimately adopt (smoothing applied only at inference time, with full training-time agency at $\alpha=1$) therefore sits at the opposite pole from CAPS: rather than injecting smoothness into the training objective, it leaves training untouched and applies smoothness only as a deployment-time post-process. We conjecture, without verifying, that the key difference between the two settings is reward density — robotic control receives a dense, continuous reward each step, whereas this setting's reward is dominated by a sparse, delayed transaction-cost penalty.

### 2.3 Classical portfolio optimization baselines

We compare against four classical, non-learned strategies as part of the robustness suite in Section 6, alongside the equal-weight benchmark: buy-and-hold, cross-sectional momentum, mean-variance optimization using the Ledoit & Wolf (2004) shrinkage covariance estimator (chosen over sample covariance because the asset universe is small relative to the observation window and highly cross-correlated, a setting sample covariance handles poorly), and risk parity (inverse-volatility weighting). Markowitz (1952) and Black & Litterman (1992) are the canonical references for mean-variance and Bayesian-prior portfolio construction respectively; our MVO baseline follows the unconstrained global-minimum-variance formulation rather than the full mean-variance frontier, since forward-return estimates in this setting are noisy enough that the minimum-variance corner is the more defensible choice. Separately, the top-$K$ sparse selection of Section 4.4 can be read as a differentiable relaxation of cardinality-constrained portfolio optimization from the combinatorial-optimization literature — adding an upper bound on the number of held assets to the mean-variance model (Chang, Meade, Beasley, & Sharaiha, 2000; exact algorithms in Bertsimas & Shioda, 2009) — approximated here by a learned confidence score rather than solved exactly. The Spearman IC diagnostic in Section 6.8, however, shows this learned approximation barely predicts the cross-sectional ranking of future returns, suggesting the "selection based on genuine signal" assumption underlying the classical formulation may not hold for this neural approximation.

### 2.4 Reinforcement-learning algorithms

All specialist and router policies in this paper are trained with off-the-shelf actor-critic and value-based methods rather than a novel RL algorithm: Proximal Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2017) for the continuous-weight specialist pool, and standard Deep Q-Networks (Mnih et al., 2015) for the discrete regime router. The original EarnHFT paper uses DDQN (van Hasselt, Guez, & Silver, 2016) for both Stage I pool training and the Stage III router; this paper substitutes standard DQN for both, for implementation simplicity — a departure from, not a match to, EarnHFT's choice. Section 8 (Limitations) separately discusses what this simplification could contribute to the router-collapse diagnosis in Section 4.5. The contribution of this paper is not to the RL algorithms themselves but to the architecture built on top of them and the diagnostic methodology used to evaluate it.

### 2.5 Backtest-overfitting diagnostics

The closest work to Sections 4.5–4.6 and Section 6 of this paper is Gort, Liu, Sun, Gao, Chen, & Wang (2022; arXiv:2209.05559), who study exactly the failure mode we encounter — a deep RL cryptocurrency trading agent whose backtest performance does not reflect a genuine, reproducible edge — and propose formalizing overfitting detection as a hypothesis test over repeated train/evaluate cycles. Our validation/test Sharpe-gap diagnostic (Section 4.6) began as a simpler, qualitative version of the same idea, and Section 6.10 formalizes it directly as a block permutation test, confirming it quantitatively as well — consistent in direction but short of conventional significance. Section 6 argues this kind of diagnostic should be reported as a statistical test rather than an ad hoc comparison in future work along these lines.

Two tools from the quantitative finance literature give us a way to formalize "how much should a single good backtest be trusted": the **Probability of Backtest Overfitting** (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado, & Zhu, 2016, *Journal of Computational Finance*; SSRN 2326253), which estimates overfitting probability via combinatorially symmetric cross-validation, and the **Deflated Sharpe Ratio** (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014, *Journal of Portfolio Management*), which corrects a Sharpe ratio for the number of independent trials from which it was selected and for non-normality of returns. We apply the latter, rather than PBO, to our 16-seed sweep in Section 6.2: PBO's combinatorially symmetric cross-validation is built to test a single continuous track record by partitioning it into many in-sample/out-of-sample sub-period combinations, whereas our selection problem is choosing the best of $N$ independently-trained candidates (seeds) — a framing DSR's multiple-comparisons correction addresses directly without requiring a sub-period partitioning scheme; adapting PBO's combinatorial logic to a set of independently-trained candidates rather than sub-periods of one series is left for future work. We note there — as an empirical, not merely theoretical, finding — that the Deflated Sharpe Ratio corrects for a *different* form of selection bias than the validation/test consistency check used in Section 4.6: the two diagnostics are complementary rather than substitutable, and a policy can look identical under one while looking very different under the other.

### 2.6 Positioning relative to related frameworks

The table below summarizes where this paper's method sits relative to the frameworks discussed above, verified directly against each paper's published text rather than assumed (note: table numbering in this paper restarts at Table 1 in Section 5, since this positioning table is a Related Work summary rather than a results table). Two comparisons are worth calling out explicitly. First, DeepClair's own conclusion states that accounting for transaction fees is future work for that paper ("we aim to explore more realistic and robust experimental settings [including] accounting for transaction fees" — Choi et al., 2024, Section 7); by contrast, this paper's central diagnostic finding (Section 4.1) is that transaction costs are the dominant failure mode in exactly this problem class, and the deployed policy's robustness to a 0.5–2x cost range is a direct product of taking costs seriously from the outset (Section 6.9). Second, none of the four related frameworks report a validation/test consistency check in the sense used throughout this paper — EarnHFT's Stage II does select agents on a held-out validation split, which is the closest precedent, but does not report a validation/test performance gap as an explicit robustness metric; FreQuant and DeepClair each evaluate on a single held-out test window per market, with additional cross-market datasets substituting for cross-period robustness checks.

## 3. Problem Setup and Data

### 3.1 Two markets, one recurring problem

We study two independently constructed portfolio-allocation problems that share a common design pattern (an embedding backbone feeding a state space, a softmax-family action space over portfolio weights, and a PPO-trained policy) but differ in every empirical detail:

| | KOSPI equities | Cryptocurrency (Binance) |
|---|---|---|
| Universe | 781 KOSPI-listed stocks | 44 USDT spot pairs (of an initially larger set, after excluding 6 symbols with <6 months of history) |
| Bar frequency | Daily | 5-minute |
| Raw features | OHLCV + 20 technical indicators (SMA/EMA/MACD/RSI/Bollinger/ATR family) | Identical 20-indicator set, computed on the 5-minute grid |
| Sequence encoding | RevIN-normalized 60-step patches | Same patching scheme, applied per 5-minute bar |
| Prediction target | 1-day / 5-day forward return | 6-bar (30-minute) forward return, matching the live rebalancing cadence |
| State aggregation | 5 DTW k-means clusters (of 757 clustered stocks); state = per-cluster mean embedding | Per-asset; state = per-asset embedding (no clustering) |
| Action space | 5-dimensional (cluster weights) | Up to 45-dimensional (asset weights [+ cash], see Section 3.3) |
| Training / test window | 2021–2023 / 2024–2025 | 2025 (full year, or a 1–9 month subset, depending on experiment) / 2026 H1 |

The two universes were built independently (different data vendors, different feature pipelines, different clustering vs. no-clustering design decisions) before either track's RL component was designed. This independence is what makes the shared single-policy failure mode in Section 4.1 informative: it was not produced by copying one experimental setup onto the other. How these design differences bear on the interpretation of the shared failure — and exactly where this paper's generality claims stop — is addressed directly in Section 7.

A further asymmetry matters more than the bar-frequency row of the table above suggests on its own: EarnHFT's original setting (Section 2.1) operates at second-level decision frequency, and both tracks here run several orders of magnitude slower than that — crypto's 30-minute rebalancing cadence already falls well outside what the original paper calls high-frequency, and KOSPI's daily cadence is slower still. This matters most for KOSPI: at a once-a-day rebalancing cadence, the transaction-cost drag that motivates EarnHFT's hierarchical structure in the first place (Section 4.1) has essentially no room to compound the way it does at 30-minute or sub-second intervals. What this means for how the KOSPI results in Section 7 should and should not be read is addressed directly there and in Limitation 1 of Section 8.

### 3.2 Common embedding backbone

Both tracks use the same family of per-asset (or per-cluster) embedding backbone, selected after a systematic comparison of eight candidate time-series backbones on directional accuracy and Spearman rank correlation of return forecasts (StockMixer, PatchTST, iTransformer, Chronos zero-shot, Chronos-LoRA, MTGNN, Mamba/BiGRU, and a Chronos+MTGNN hybrid). The zero-shot Chronos encoder (Ansari et al., 2024) was the strongest single backbone on directional accuracy, and MTGNN (Wu et al., 2020) — an adaptive-graph-learner plus gated temporal convolution originally designed for multivariate forecasting — was the strongest on the extended comparison; the deployed backbone combines a Chronos T5 encoder (temporal representation per asset) with an MTGNN-style cross-feature attention layer (variate/cross-asset representation), following the design pattern of iTransformer (Liu et al., 2023) for the cross-variate component. The backbone emits a 64-dimensional embedding per asset (or per cluster, in the KOSPI case) per rebalancing step; this embedding, concatenated with the current portfolio weight vector, is the full state observed by the RL policy in every variant described below. Full backbone comparison numbers and RevIN normalization details (Kim et al., 2022) are reported in the project's supplementary technical log (outside the scope of this paper) and are not repeated here since the embedding backbone itself is held fixed across all RL experiments in this paper — none of the failure modes in Section 4 are attributable to the backbone.

### 3.3 Common environment formalism

Unless otherwise noted, every environment variant in Section 4 shares the following structure. At each step $t$, the agent observes a state $s_t = [\,e_{1,t}, \dots, e_{N,t},\, w_{t-1}\,] \in \mathbb{R}^{N \cdot d + N}$, where $N$ is the number of assets (or clusters), $d = 64$ is the embedding dimension, $e_{i,t}$ is asset $i$'s embedding, and $w_{t-1}$ is the portfolio weight vector carried over from the previous step (for the 44-asset crypto case, $N{\cdot}d + N = 2{,}860$; variants with an explicit cash leg add one further dimension for the previous cash weight). Each embedding $e_{i,t}$ is encoded from data up to and including step $t$ only, keeping the backbone's inputs strictly separated from the forward-looking target return used in the reward below. The policy outputs an action $a_t$, which is mapped to a target weight vector $w_t$ (softmax normalization in the dense variants of Section 4.1–4.3; the top-$K$-plus-gate transform of Section 4.4 in the sparse variant). The environment then charges a proportional transaction cost on the realized turnover,

$$\text{cost}_t = \tau \cdot \lVert w_t - w_{t-1} \rVert_1,$$

with $\tau = 0.001$ (10 bps), matching Binance's standard taker fee. This is a linear, fee-only cost model: it does not capture order-book slippage or nonlinear market impact, which can plausibly exceed 10bp at times for a 44-asset book rebalanced every 30 minutes. Section 6.9 tests robustness to a mis-specified linear fee up to 2x the assumed rate, but that sweep does not substitute for a full market-impact model. The environment computes the step reward as the realized portfolio return net of this cost,

$$r_t = w_t^\top \rho_t - \text{cost}_t,$$

where $\rho_t$ is the vector of realized forward asset returns for step $t$ (the pre-computed 6-bar target return described in Section 3.1, so no lookahead is introduced). All policies are trained with PPO (`stable-baselines3`, `MlpPolicy`) using a common hyperparameter budget of learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, $n_\text{steps}=1024$, batch size 64, 10 epochs per update, $\gamma=0.99$, GAE $\lambda=0.95$, clip range 0.2, entropy coefficient 0.01, and value-function coefficient 0.5, over 100k environment steps per specialist agent; the DQN router (Section 4.1, 4.5) uses learning rate $10^{-3}$, replay buffer size 20,000, batch size 64, $\gamma=0.95$, a $[128,64]$ MLP, and $\epsilon$-greedy exploration annealed from 0.3 to 0.05, over a 20k-step budget. These budgets are held fixed across v1–v4 specifically so that the architectural comparisons in Section 4 and Section 5 are not confounded by unequal compute.

## 4. Method: An Architecture Cascade

Each subsection below corresponds to one iteration in the cascade summarized in Section 1.2. We describe the environment or training change first, then the outcome and diagnosis; quantitative results are consolidated in Section 5's ablation tables rather than repeated in full here.

### 4.1 v1 — Direct EarnHFT port without a Q-teacher

Stage A segments the 2025–2026 market history into regimes by taking local extrema of a low-pass-filtered price series and merging them (via DTW-based similarity) into contiguous chunks labeled with one of five regimes — `bear`, `pullback`, `sideways`, `rally`, `bull` — yielding 187 chunks of roughly 3 days each, reasonably balanced across labels. Stage B trains four specialist PPO agents over the dense 44-dimensional softmax action space of Section 3.3, one per risk-preference setting $\beta \in \{-90, -30, 30, 90\}$; in the absence of a DP teacher, $\beta$ is used only to bias the *sampling priority* of training chunks toward regimes where a preference of that sign would historically have been favorable (`chunk_sampling_priority`, with a fixed risk threshold of 0.2), leaving the PPO objective itself unchanged. Stage C trains a DQN router (Section 3.3 hyperparameters) over macro observations to select among the four specialists at each new chunk boundary.

The resulting system loses 32.24% of capital net of trading costs over the test window. Recomputing the same trajectory of actions without the 10 bps transaction cost recovers +12.6% — already ahead of the +2.66% equal-weight benchmark — and the per-step turnover in the raw system averages 5.4% of portfolio value, compounding to a cost drag of −39.81% over the window. The signal produced by Stage B+C is therefore real by this measure; it is destroyed entirely by 30-minute-cadence rebalancing costs before it reaches net returns.

### 4.2 Inference-time vs. training-time inertia

The most direct fix for a cost-dominated policy is to reduce turnover. We test two ways of doing this. The first, applied only at inference time with no retraining, blends each step's target weights into the previous weights via a fixed-rate exponential filter,

$$w_t = (1-\alpha)\, w_{t-1} + \alpha \cdot \text{softmax}(a_t),$$

with $\alpha$ swept over $[0.01, 0.5]$; the best setting, $\alpha=0.01$, brings the same trained policy from −32.24% to +11.05% net of costs (Sharpe 0.638, MDD −36.47%), while leaving the cost-free alpha essentially unchanged (12.5–12.6% across the swept range) — evidence that the underlying signal operates at a multi-day, not 30-minute, frequency, and that inertia simply lets the policy express it without over-trading.

The second approach bakes the same exponential blending directly into the training environment (a `SmoothStepMixin` with $\alpha=0.03$), on the reasoning that a policy trained to expect its own actions to be damped might learn to compensate, or might learn an inherently lower-turnover policy from the start (as discussed in Section 2.2, this is an independent rediscovery of the same idea as CAPS's (Mysore, Mabsout, Mancuso, & Saenko, 2021) training-time smoothness regularization for robotic control, applied to this setting). This is worse than doing nothing: net return falls to −3.58%, and — more informatively — the cost-free alpha collapses from 12.6% to 0.92% — the opposite of the result CAPS reports for robotic control. We hypothesize this reflects a credit-assignment failure — once the environment itself decouples the executed weight change from the raw action the policy emits, PPO's advantage estimates may no longer point toward actions that helped, degrading the policy's incentive gradient — though we note this mechanism is an interpretation of the outcome, not confirmed by a direct diagnostic such as advantage variance or policy loss, which we did not collect for this run. The practical rule this establishes, and which we carry through the remainder of the paper, is: **train with full agency ($\alpha=1$ during training), and apply inertia only at execution time.**

### 4.3 v3 — A more faithful reimplementation still lacks a diversity engine

To rule out that v1's simplifications (no cash action, priority-sampling instead of a teacher, and test-set-tuned agent selection) were themselves responsible for the shortfall, we build a closer reimplementation of EarnHFT's Stage II/III selection procedure. A 45th action dimension is added representing cash (return 0, no transaction cost on cash-only rebalancing), giving agents with negative $\beta$ a direct way to express de-risking rather than only reallocating among risky assets. The 2025 calendar year is split strictly into a 9-month training period and a 3-month (Q4) validation period (4,416 30-minute rebalancing steps); 20 candidate agents (4 values of $\beta$ $\times$ 5 training checkpoints) are each evaluated per-regime on the validation split, and the best candidate is retained per regime, following the original paper's Stage II procedure. The router and the final $\alpha$ smoothing rate are likewise selected on this validation split rather than on the test period, removing the test-set selection bias present in v1 and its inertia fix.

This version still fails: +0.68% net of costs, below the +2.65% equal-weight benchmark. Inspecting the deployed policy shows the cash weight pinned at 2.2% — exactly $1/45$, the uniform-initialization value — for essentially the entire test window, and the 20 candidate agents' regime-conditional validation scores differ by under one percentage point from each other, i.e., they behave as near-identical clones despite having been trained with four different $\beta$ values. We interpret this as confirming the role of EarnHFT's Stage I directly: risk preference expressed only through the *sampling distribution* of training data, with the PPO objective itself unchanged, is not sufficient to produce distinguishable behavior in a 45-dimensional continuous action space — without a teacher signal that injects $\beta$-dependent targets directly into the loss, gradient descent has no reason to move away from a uniform-weight local optimum in either direction.

Rather than leave this causal claim as a post-hoc interpretation, we test it directly with a minimal intervention. Leaving `CashPortfolioEnv` unchanged, we add a single scalar auxiliary term directly to the reward that pushes cash weight in a $\beta$-sign-dependent direction ($\text{aux} = -\lambda \cdot (\beta/90) \cdot (\text{cash} - 0.5)$) — not a reproduction of the full DP-based $Q^*_\beta$ teacher, but a minimal transplant of its core idea that the *target itself*, not just the data distribution, should differ by $\beta$. Retraining while increasing the auxiliary strength $\lambda$ 15-fold (0.01 $\to$ 0.15), the Spearman rank correlation between $\beta$ order and mean cash-weight order improves monotonically: $+0.400$ (original v3, the wrong direction) $\to$ $-0.400$ ($\lambda=0.01$) $\to$ $-0.800$ ($\lambda=0.15$). This dose-response relationship — obtained by manipulating a single variable (signal strength) and observing a response in the predicted direction — confirms that the collapse is controllable via the diagnosed mechanism (objective-level signal vs. sampling distribution) rather than an artifact of a single run or an implementation-specific tuning failure, and is consistent with the objective-level diversity-injection principle established by FeUdal Networks and DIAYN (Eysenbach et al., 2019; Section 2.2). Restored diversity does not, however, guarantee improved performance: evaluating the $\lambda=0.15$ pool with the same inference-time smoothing as Section 4.2 shows all four specialists performing near equal-weight on validation, and the candidate with the flashiest raw test return ($\beta=-30$, +8.64%) has the worst validation/test Sharpe gap of the four (0.850) — the same overfitting pattern confirmed in Section 4.6, reproduced here in this reduced-scope experiment.

### 4.4 v4 — Restoring diversity with a structural action-space change

Rather than attempt to reintroduce a DP-style teacher (intractable at this scale and asset count — see Section 8), we instead change the shape of the action space itself, adapting the two mechanisms described in Section 2.2. `SparseGatedCryptoPortfolioEnv` takes a $(N{+}1)$-dimensional action: the first $N$ components are per-asset confidence logits, and the last is a single risk-gate logit. The transform first selects the top-$K$ ($K=10$; held fixed throughout Section 4, swept post hoc in Section 6.8) assets by confidence logit and applies softmax within that subset only (adapting FreQuant's Eq. 13–14), then scales the resulting sub-portfolio by $\sigma(\text{gate logit}) \in (0,1)$ (adapting DeepClair's exposure gate $\rho$); the remainder of the portfolio is implicitly held in cash. Stage A's regime chunks and $\beta$-conditioned sampling priority are reused unchanged from v1, and the specialist and router training budgets are held identical to v1 (Section 3.3), so that the only variable changed relative to v1 is the shape of the action space.

A three-part diversity diagnostic — the time-series standard deviation of the risk gate $\rho$, the mean $\rho$ conditioned on $\beta$, and the number of distinct assets appearing in any step's top-10 holdings over the test window — is run before evaluating performance, specifically to check whether v3's collapse reproduces. It does not: $\rho$'s standard deviation is 0.068 (effectively zero in v3), mean $\rho$ ranges from 0.109 ($\beta=-30$) to 0.545 ($\beta=90$) — a clear, though not perfectly monotonic, separation by risk preference — and the top-10 holdings rotate through 33 of the 44 available assets over the test window. Diversity is structurally restored. Performance, however, is mixed: the *raw* (unsmoothed) policy pool has an essentially zero pre-cost alpha (−0.60%), meaning the low-level sparse-and-gated policy alone is weaker than v1's dense policy before any smoothing is applied. Applying the same $\alpha=0.01$ inference-time smoothing from Section 4.2 yields +8.26% net of costs, Sharpe 2.168, MDD −5.22% — lower total return than v1+smoothing (+11.05%, Sharpe 0.638, MDD −36.47%) but a markedly better risk-adjusted profile. Unlike v1 (Section 4.2), where pre-cost alpha was invariant to $\alpha$ (12.5–12.6% across the swept range, so smoothing only ever avoided destroying an existing signal), v4's pre-cost alpha itself moves with $\alpha$: the smoothed policy's own pre-cost return is +8.91%, not the −0.60% of the raw policy quoted above. Inference-time smoothing is therefore not merely preserving a fixed pre-existing signal here, as it does for v1 — it changes which trades are realized in a way that produces a positive pre-cost return where the unsmoothed policy has essentially none. We do not have a mechanistic account of why this particular smoothing filter recovers a pre-cost edge from what looks like near-noise in the raw policy, and report this as an open question rather than a demonstrated causal story (Section 6.8's near-zero rank correlation between the top-$K$ confidence signal and realized returns is a related piece of the same puzzle, discovered independently). A separate diagnostic rules out that this low-turnover result is a static-position artifact (turnover does not decay to zero by the end of the test window, and both the first and second half of the window contribute positive return, +5.47% and +2.64% respectively, though this front-loading is noted as an open concern in Section 8).

### 4.5 The "matched" router and its collapse

The router in Section 4.4 observes, among other macro features, the fraction of the current regime chunk elapsed (`episode_progress`) — a quantity that is well-defined during training, where each chunk is a bounded episode, but has no natural definition at live-inference time, where there is no episode boundary; the deployed system approximates it with a constant 0.5. To remove this train/serve mismatch, we retrain only Stage C (the router) with `episode_progress` fixed to 0.5 during training as well, reusing the unchanged Stage B specialist pool from Section 4.4 — a "matched" router. A single run of this matched system appears to improve on Section 4.4's already-strong result: net return moves from +8.26% to +7.48% (a small decrease) while Sharpe rises from 2.168 to 3.046 and MDD improves from −5.22% to −3.33%. At this point in the investigation this looked like a successful fix for a genuine train/serve inconsistency.

A follow-up ablation — designed to check *why* the fix helped, rather than simply accepting that it did — instead shows that it did not help at all. Inspecting the matched router's selection log shows it selects the $\beta=30$ specialist on 100% of both validation-period and test-period chunks (195/195 test chunks; 92/92 validation chunks — see Section 4.6). Running the $\beta=30$ specialist alone, with no router, on both periods reproduces the matched-router numbers to four decimal places (test: +7.4776% both ways; validation: −0.1938% both ways). The router had not learned to route market-conditionally; matching the training and serving distributions of `episode_progress` had, in this instance, caused Stage C's learning process to collapse onto a single constant action, discarding all of its macro inputs. The apparent Sharpe/MDD improvement reported above was not a property of the router's decision-making — it was a property of the $\beta=30$ specialist alone, which the previous (unmatched) router had also been selecting on a plurality, but not entirety, of test-period chunks.

### 4.6 Which specialist, and is its test performance real?

This discovery reframes the remaining question: is the $\beta=30$ specialist's standalone performance — inherited unchanged, it turns out, from the collapsed router — actually good, or does it merely look good on this particular test window? We answer this by running all four Stage-B specialists standalone (no router at all) over both the validation period (2025 Q4) and the test period (2026 H1):

| $\beta$ | Validation Sharpe | Test Sharpe | Validation return | Test return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| −90 | 0.416 | 0.600 | +2.23% | +4.74% |
| **−30** | **1.314** | **1.156** | **+4.54%** | **+5.66%** |
| 30 | 0.025 | 3.046 | −0.19% | +7.48% |
| 90 | −0.779 | −1.063 | −16.68% | −23.00% |
| Equal-weight | −0.444 | 0.367 | −29.57% | +2.66% |

Three of the four specialists behave consistently across the two periods — $\beta=90$ fails in both (a policy poorly matched to the realized regime mix), and $\beta=-90$ and $\beta=-30$ succeed, moderately and strongly respectively, in both. Only $\beta=30$ shows a large swing, from a validation Sharpe indistinguishable from zero (0.025) to the highest test Sharpe of any specialist (3.046) — a nearly two-order-of-magnitude jump that no other specialist exhibits under an otherwise identical evaluation procedure. Because the swing is specific to one specialist rather than shared across all four, it cannot be explained by a validation/test regime shift affecting the whole market (which would be expected to move all four specialists' Sharpe ratios in a correlated way); the more parsimonious explanation is that $\beta=30$'s test-period result reflects overfitting or chance rather than a validation-verifiable edge.

We take the **validation-to-test Sharpe gap** — 0.184 for $\beta=-90$, **0.158 for $\beta=-30$**, 3.021 for $\beta=30$, and 0.284 for $\beta=90$ — as a diagnostic in its own right, orthogonal to the raw magnitude of test-period performance, and select $\beta=-30$ for deployment on this basis: it is not the specialist with the highest test-period return or Sharpe, but the one whose performance is most consistent across a period it was not selected on. This selection is reinforced, not merely a close call decided by gap alone: $\beta=-30$'s validation Sharpe (1.314) is also the highest of the four specialists by a wide margin (next-best 0.416), so the choice reflects both the smallest gap and the strongest standalone validation-period performance. The final deployed policy is therefore the $\beta=-30$ specialist alone, with no router, combined with the $\alpha=0.01$ inference-time smoothing filter from Section 4.2 — the simplest configuration in the entire cascade, arrived at only after three more complex configurations (v1's router, v3's cash-augmented selection, v4's matched router) were each shown, by a targeted ablation, to not be doing the work their surface-level metrics suggested. Section 6 subjects this final configuration to the robustness battery summarized in Section 1.3.

## 6. Robustness Analysis

Having selected $\beta=-30$ on the basis of a single validation/test comparison (Section 4.6), we treat that selection itself as a claim requiring independent verification, and subject it to five additional checks.

### 6.1 Multi-seed reproducibility

We retrain the deployed configuration ($\beta=-30$, top-$K$+gate specialist, $\alpha=0.01$ smoothing, no router) from five additional random seeds (11, 22, 33, 44, 55), holding architecture and training budget fixed:

| Seed | Validation return | Validation Sharpe | Test return | Test Sharpe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **42 (deployed)** | +4.54% | **1.314** | +5.66% | 1.156 |
| 11 | +7.01% | 0.706 | +36.67% | 2.567 |
| 22 | +6.27% | 1.024 | +3.76% | 0.827 |
| 33 | −0.53% | 0.118 | +0.48% | 0.134 |
| 44 | −0.45% | 0.204 | +11.68% | 1.414 |
| 55 | −2.44% | −0.368 | +4.80% | 1.045 |
| Equal-weight | −29.57% | −0.444 | +2.66% | 0.367 |

**Direction reproduces in 6/6 seeds**: every seed beats the equal-weight benchmark in both periods (seed 55's validation Sharpe is negative in absolute terms but still far above equal-weight's −0.444). **Magnitude does not reproduce**: test Sharpe across the five new seeds has mean 1.197 and standard deviation 0.896 — a coefficient of variation near 1. Seeds 11 and 44 show large validation-to-test jumps (0.706→2.567 and 0.204→1.414) reminiscent of the $\beta=30$ overfitting pattern in Section 4.6, though less extreme. Seed 42 — the seed already in production — has the smallest validation/test gap (0.158) of all six, meaning the deployment decision, made before this sweep was run, turned out to have selected the most internally consistent seed available.

To address the concern that six seeds is a thin sample for this kind of claim, we extend the sweep with ten further seeds (66, 77, 88, 99, 110, 121, 132, 143, 154, 165), for a total $N=16$. Six of the ten new seeds have positive validation Sharpe and nine of ten have positive test Sharpe (only seed 66 is negative, at −0.397); the new seeds' test Sharpe has mean 0.907 and standard deviation 0.766 across the 15 non-deployed seeds — a similar spread to the 5-seed sample, suggesting the six-seed sample was not unrepresentatively narrow. Seed 42 retains the highest validation Sharpe of all 16 candidates (1.314; second-best is seed 22 at 1.024), reinforcing rather than overturning the original seed-selection outcome.

### 6.2 Deflated Sharpe Ratio

We apply the Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014; Section 2.5) to the seed sweep, treating each seed as one trial in a multiple-comparisons correction, and compare the deployed seed (42) against the seed with the single highest raw test Sharpe (11):

| Candidate | Test Sharpe | DSR ($N=6$) | DSR ($N=16$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seed 42 (deployed) | 1.156 | 0.533 | 0.447 |
| Seed 11 (raw maximum) | 2.567 | 0.866 | 0.815 |

Two findings run counter to an initial, intuitive expectation. First, we had expected the seed with the highest raw Sharpe (11) to be *penalized more* by a multiple-comparisons correction on the reasoning that "the maximum of many trials is the least trustworthy one" — instead, its DSR is higher than the deployed seed's in both settings. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio's correction threshold $SR^*$ is a function of the *number of trials* and is shared across all candidates drawn from that trial set; DSR then measures whether each candidate's *own* Sharpe clears that common, trial-count-dependent bar, not whether the candidate was itself the maximum. A candidate with a higher raw Sharpe clears a common bar more easily, so multiple-comparisons correction and "distrust the maximum" are not the same operation — an easy conflation that this result exposes empirically. Second, extending $N$ from 6 to 16 lowers both candidates' DSR (0.533→0.447 and 0.866→0.815) as $SR^*$ rises with trial count, exactly as expected, but does not change the qualitative conclusion: neither candidate clears the conventional 0.95 significance threshold at either sample size, meaning tripling the number of trials does not rescue a claim of formal statistical significance that a 6-seed sample also could not support. We read this as evidence that the $N=6$ result was not an artifact of an unusually small sample, and that the deployment argument for seed 42 must rest on the validation/test consistency of Section 6.1, not on DSR significance, which neither candidate achieves.

We emphasize, following Section 2.5, that the Deflated Sharpe Ratio and the validation/test Sharpe gap answer different questions: DSR asks whether a single test-period Sharpe ratio survives correction for having been chosen as the best of $N$ trials *within that one period*; the validation/test gap asks whether a policy's quality holds when the *evaluation period itself* changes. A policy can pass one check and fail the other — $\beta=30$ (Section 4.6) would very likely have shown a large validation-period DSR mismatch (near-zero validation Sharpe) had we applied it there, which the test-period-only DSR calculation above does not capture. Both diagnostics are reported because neither subsumes the other.

### 6.3 Leakage-free clean-validation retraining

Every hyperparameter selection reported so far — including $\alpha$ and, implicitly, the choice among the four $\beta$ specialists — was made by evaluating on a period that overlaps with data available at the time of the original architecture-selection experiments (2025 Q4), raising the concern that "validation" was not fully held out in the traditional sense. To test this directly, we retrain $\beta=-30$ (seed 42) using **only** January–September 2025 for training (the same boundary used in Section 4.3's v3 experiment), sweep $\alpha$ on the truly-unseen October–December 2025 window, and run the resulting configuration on the 2026 H1 test period exactly once:

| | Test return | Test Sharpe | MDD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equal-weight benchmark | +2.66% | 0.367 | −40.09% |
| Deployed (trained on full 2025, $\alpha$ selected on the same period used for architecture selection) | +5.66% | 1.156 | −7.43% |
| **Clean retrain (trained on 9 months, $\alpha$ selected on genuinely unseen 3 months)** | **+7.83%** | **1.393** | −8.73% |

The clean-retrain configuration — with less training data and a stricter separation between selection and evaluation — outperforms the originally deployed configuration rather than underperforming it. This argues against the hypothesis that validation-period exposure inflated the deployed model's reported performance: if it had, we would expect the leakage-free variant to score *lower*, not higher. We do not switch the deployed policy to this clean-retrain variant, since doing so on the strength of a single test-period comparison would reintroduce exactly the test-set-informed selection bias this study otherwise tries to avoid; the clean retrain is reported solely as a check on the credibility of the already-deployed result, and it passes.

### 6.4 Ensemble averaging (a negative result)

A natural way to reduce the seed-to-seed variance documented in Section 6.1 is to average several seeds' portfolio weights at each step rather than deploying a single one. We test this with the original six seeds (42 and the five from Section 6.1), with no additional training:

| Configuration | Validation Sharpe | Test Sharpe | \|Valid − Test\| gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seed 33 | 0.118 | 0.134 | 0.016 (smallest, but weak absolute performance) |
| **Seed 42 (deployed)** | **1.314** | **1.156** | **0.158** |
| Seed 22 | 1.024 | 0.827 | 0.198 |
| Ensemble of 5 (excluding seed 11) | 0.386 | 1.013 | 0.627 |
| Ensemble of all 6 | 0.501 | 1.599 | 1.098 |
| Seed 44 | 0.204 | 1.414 | 1.210 |
| Seed 55 | −0.368 | 1.045 | 1.413 |
| Seed 11 | 0.706 | 2.567 | 1.861 |

Both ensembles have a *larger* validation/test gap than the single deployed seed — the full 6-seed ensemble's gap (1.098) is nearly seven times seed 42's (0.158), and even excluding the most volatile seed (11) does not bring the 5-seed ensemble's gap below seed 42's. We had expected averaging across seeds trained with independent random initializations to behave somewhat like averaging independent noise, reducing variance; it does not, in this setting. A plausible explanation, which we did not verify further, is that the specialists' top-$K$ asset selections differ enough from seed to seed that a weight-space average dilutes each seed's sparse, concentrated position into a more diffuse one that resembles neither seed's original (and validated) risk profile — but we report this as an open, unverified hypothesis rather than a confirmed mechanism. The practical conclusion is unambiguous regardless of mechanism: **ensembling seeds by weight-averaging does not improve robustness in this setting**, and the single-seed deployment remains the best-supported choice among the alternatives tested.

### 6.5 Classical baselines

We compare the deployed policy against four baselines that involve no learning: buy-and-hold (no rebalancing after the initial allocation), cross-sectional momentum (daily rebalancing into the top-10 assets by trailing 7-day return), mean-variance optimization using Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage covariance (weekly rebalancing, unconstrained global minimum variance, no short-selling), and risk parity (daily rebalancing to inverse-volatility weights, using a trailing 20-step realized-volatility estimate per asset). Results are reported in Table 2 (Section 5) and repeated here for the discussion: momentum achieves the highest raw test return in the entire study (+106.04%) but also the largest validation/test Sharpe gap (2.742); MVO fails outright, scoring similarly to or worse than the equal-weight benchmark in both periods (−9.10% test, −29.40% validation) — a negative result we attribute to the high pairwise correlation typical of cryptocurrency assets, under which covariance-based optimization has little exploitable structure to work with. Risk parity fails in the same direction as MVO and by a similar margin (−13.60% test, −29.06% validation) — inverse-volatility weighting offers no more exploitable structure here than covariance-based optimization does — but it also has the single smallest validation/test Sharpe gap of any strategy tested in this paper (0.003), smaller even than the deployed policy's (0.158). We flag this deliberately: risk parity is the clearest counterexample in this study to the idea that a small validation/test gap is, by itself, evidence of a good policy — it is only informative jointly with a positive raw result, exactly the two-part criterion Section 4.6 and Section 7 build on. Of the six strategies compared (equal-weight, buy-and-hold, momentum, MVO, risk parity, and the deployed RL policy), the deployed policy is the only one with a positive Sharpe ratio in both the validation and the test period.

### 6.6 A deep-learning baseline: EIIE

To rule out that the RL policy's advantage over classical baselines merely reflects the value of *any* portfolio-management neural network, we implement EIIE (Jiang, Xu, & Liang, 2017) — the network topology of a shared per-asset CNN evaluator, a portfolio-vector memory, and a cash bias, producing softmax portfolio weights — trained with this paper's PPO pipeline rather than the original paper's online stochastic batch learning (OSBL) procedure, using raw OHLC price windows with no embedding backbone, under the same bucket structure, transaction cost, and 100k-step training budget as $\beta=-30$. EIIE scores 0.275 test Sharpe, statistically indistinguishable from the recomputed equal-weight benchmark in this notebook (0.330) and similarly close to equal-weight in validation (−0.969 vs. −0.982$^\ddagger$) — i.e., **EIIE converges to an equal-weight-like policy**, reproducing the same near-uniform collapse observed in Section 4.3's v3 experiment, in an entirely different network architecture and action parameterization. This strengthens the Section 4.3 diagnosis: the difficulty in this setting is not specific to the sparse-selection or dense-softmax action spaces used elsewhere in this paper, but appears to be a more general property of training a portfolio-weighting policy from scratch, without an external diversity-inducing signal, on this asset universe and data budget. The deployed $\beta=-30$ policy outperforms EIIE by roughly a factor of four in Sharpe ratio (1.156 vs. 0.275), which we interpret as evidence that the combination of a pretrained embedding backbone (Section 3.2), structural action-space sparsification (Section 4.4), and inference-time smoothing (Section 4.2) is doing real work relative to a standard deep RL portfolio baseline, rather than simply benefiting from a stronger PPO implementation.

Since EIIE (2017) is now a relatively old point in the deep-learning portfolio-management literature, we implement a more recent attention-based architecture the same way: **LSRE-CAAN** (Li, Zhang, Yang, & Chen, 2023) — an attention-bottleneck encoder (LSRE) in which a small number of learned latents cross-attend to the price sequence, followed by a module (CAAN) that applies cross-asset attention over the resulting per-asset representations to produce final scores — trained with the same PPO pipeline and the same raw price-window input. LSRE-CAAN scores 0.332 test Sharpe, again statistically indistinguishable from the equal-weight benchmark (0.330) — a **third architecture (the 45-dimensional dense-softmax v3, the CNN-based EIIE, and the attention-based LSRE-CAAN) reproducing the same near-uniform collapse**. This further strengthens the Section 4.3/6.6 diagnosis: the diversity failure in this setting is not a product of any particular network topology, but appears to be a general property of training a portfolio-weighting policy from scratch without an external diversity-inducing signal (such as EarnHFT's Q-teacher). The deployed $\beta=-30$ policy outperforms LSRE-CAAN by roughly a factor of 3.5 in Sharpe ratio (1.156 vs. 0.332).

$^\ddagger$ See the Table 2 footnote (Section 5) regarding this notebook's independently constructed evaluation buckets.

### 6.7 Auxiliary evidence: restoring the Q-teacher restores diversity, on a single asset

Section 4.3 attributes v3's diversity collapse to the absence of EarnHFT's Stage I dynamic-programming teacher, which is intractable to reintroduce directly at the 44-asset, continuous-weight scale studied in this paper (Section 8). As an indirect check on this causal claim, we note results from a parallel, single-asset branch of this project that reimplements EarnHFT closer to its original scope — Bitcoin only, discrete long/flat/short positions, including Stage I. In this setting, where a DP-optimal teacher *is* tractable, the resulting specialist pool shows clearly separated average positions across the four $\beta$ values (ranging 0.39 to 0.79), i.e., diversity by the same measure that collapsed in Section 4.3's 44-asset, no-teacher experiment. This single-asset branch's overall returns are poor for unrelated reasons (an initial NAV accounting bug, and a rule-based router mis-calibrated to the wrong decision boundary), and its results are not otherwise comparable to the 44-asset results in this paper — we report only the diversity outcome, as corroborating evidence for the causal role of Stage I identified in Section 4.3, not as an additional performance result.

### 6.8 Top-$K$ hyperparameter sensitivity sweep

Section 4.4 fixed top-$K$ at $K=10$ for the entire v1–v4 comparison (part of the general policy, described in Section 3.3, of holding budgets equal across configurations). That this choice was made without a sweep was originally flagged as a limitation (Section 8). To check it post hoc, we hold the deployed configuration fixed ($\beta=-30$, $\alpha=0.01$ smoothing, no router) and retrain with $K \in \{5, 7, 15\}$ (reusing the Section 4.4 model for $K=10$), applying the same validation (2025 Q4) / test (2026 H1) consistency diagnostic used in Section 4.6:

| $K$ | Val. Sharpe | Test Sharpe | \|Val.−Test\| gap | Val. return | Test return |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 1.741 | 0.541 | 1.200 | +4.45% | +1.72% |
| 7 | −0.516 | 1.593 | 2.109 | −7.85% | +18.51% |
| **10 (deployed)** | **1.314** | 1.156 | **0.158** | +4.54% | +5.66% |
| 15 | −0.200 | 0.734 | 0.934 | −3.76% | +8.07% |

Of the four swept values, only the deployed $K=10$ achieves a validation/test gap below 0.2; the other three all exceed 0.9. $K=7$ in particular reproduces exactly the pattern seen with $\beta=30$ in Section 4.6: it fails outright in validation (Sharpe $-0.516$, return $-7.85\%$) yet posts the second-highest raw test return in the entire study (+18.51%, more than triple $K{=}10$'s). Applying Section 4.6's reasoning directly, this jump should be read as test-period-specific overfitting rather than a verifiable edge — choosing $K=7$ on raw test return alone would have repeated the exact mistake avoided when the $\beta=30$ specialist was rejected. **$K=10$ is therefore not an untuned, arbitrary choice, but the only value among those swept that passes this paper's consistency criterion** — though the swept range ($\{5,7,10,15\}$) does not exactly match the range originally flagged as a future candidate ($\{5,10,15,20\}$) in Section 8.

This sweep also surfaces an incidental negative result. Measuring the Spearman rank correlation (IC) between each asset's confidence logit — the signal used for top-$K$ selection — and its realized return, across all four $\beta$ specialists on the test period, yields mean IC of $0.0006$–$0.0057$ (indistinguishable from zero), a share of IC-positive steps of $50.3$–$51.5\%$ (indistinguishable from a coin flip), and no statistically significant IC for any $\beta$ except $-90$ ($t=3.33$). In other words, **there is little evidence that the confidence logits actually predict the cross-sectional ranking of next-period returns** — performance differences across $K$ are more plausibly attributable to how the number of exposed assets interacts with the risk gate $\rho$'s effective concentration (Section 4.4) than to accurate stock selection. We report this as a reassessment of the top-$K$ mechanism's value, separate from the performance result above. This connects to an open question flagged in Section 4.4: the smoothed deployed policy's own pre-cost return is positive despite this near-zero stock-selection signal, and we do not have a mechanistic account of why.

$\tau=0.001$ (10bp) in Section 3.3 matches Binance's standard taker fee at the deployed account's current scale, but VIP tiers or BNB fee discounts could lower the effective cost (Limitation 7, Section 8). Holding the deployed configuration fixed ($\beta=-30$, $\alpha=0.01$) and retraining nothing, we re-backtest at $\tau \in \{5, 10\text{ (deployed)}, 15, 20\}$bp:

| $\tau$ | Val. Sharpe | Val. return | Test Sharpe | Test return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5bp | 1.375 | +4.77% | 1.256 | +6.19% |
| 10bp (deployed) | 1.314 | +4.54% | 1.156 | +5.66% |

### 6.10 A formal hypothesis test for the validation/test gap diagnostic

As noted in Section 2.5, the validation/test Sharpe-gap diagnostic used throughout Section 4.6 has so far been a qualitative threshold comparison. We formalize it, in the spirit of Gort et al.'s (2022) hypothesis-testing formulation, as a **block permutation test**: preserving short-horizon return autocorrelation with one-day (48-step) blocks, we compute the probability, under the null hypothesis that validation and test period labels are exchangeable, of observing a $|$validation Sharpe $-$ test Sharpe$|$ at least as large as the one actually observed. We apply this to all four $\beta$ specialists:

| $\beta$ | Gap (test $-$ valid) | Permutation $p$-value |
|---|---|---|
| $-90$ | $+0.183$ | $0.923$ |
| $-30$ (deployed) | $-0.158$ | $0.948$ |
| $30$ | $+3.021$ | $\mathbf{0.282}$ |
| $90$ | $-0.284$ | $0.882$ |

$\beta=30$ has by far the smallest $p$-value of the four (0.282 vs. 0.88–0.95 for the rest) — consistent in direction with the qualitative judgment in Section 4.6. It falls well short of a conventional significance threshold ($p<0.05$), however — exactly the pattern observed for the Deflated Sharpe Ratio in Section 6.2: the direction is right, but formal statistical significance is not reached. We attribute this low power to the test's own conservatism rather than to a weak underlying effect: preserving day-level autocorrelation forces permutation at the granularity of ~194 one-day blocks rather than the raw 9,319 steps, and daily crypto Sharpe ratios are themselves high-variance, so a two-sided test with only four $\beta$ candidates compared has limited ability to reach $p<0.05$ on a single test window even when the underlying gap is real — the same limitation, driven by short sample windows, noted for walk-forward validation in Limitation 5. This corroborates, via an independent formal test, the position already stated in Limitation 6 (Section 8): the deployment argument rests on validation/test consistency — which the diagnostic has already demonstrated in practice by catching the $\beta=30$, KOSPI, and momentum overfitting cases (Sections 4.6, 7, 6.5) — not on this permutation test achieving formal statistical significance.

![Figure 2: Validation/test consistency across every swept configuration](figures/fig2_valid_test_consistency.png)

**Figure 2.** Every configuration in Section 6 for which both a validation-period and a test-period Sharpe ratio were computed, plotted against the $y=x$ diagonal (shaded band: $|$gap$|<0.3$). The deployed $\beta=-30$ policy sits almost exactly on the diagonal; $\beta=30$, momentum, and the raw-Sharpe-maximizing seed 11 all sit far above it despite strong test-period numbers; the KOSPI generalization candidate from Section 7 sits far below it in the opposite direction. The risk-parity baseline sits essentially on the diagonal as well (gap 0.003, the smallest in the paper) yet both of its Sharpe ratios are negative — a reminder that diagonal proximity alone is not a sufficient success criterion (Section 6.5). Proximity to the diagonal, not the height of any single point, is what this paper's deployment argument is built on.

## 7. Multi-Market Analysis

Section 1.1 opened with a cross-market observation: two experiments with unrelated data pipelines, state designs, and action spaces (Korean equities, cryptocurrency) both showed a single-policy PPO agent failing to beat its equal-weight benchmark. Here we revisit that observation, then report a **third, independent market check (U.S. large-cap equities)** carried out later in this project, and finally synthesize all three to state precisely what they do, and do not, establish about the generality of this paper's findings.

**What two markets support.** The KOSPI and crypto single-policy experiments were designed independently, at different times, by different reasoning about state representation (five DTW-based clusters vs. 44 individual assets) and over non-overlapping calendar periods (2021–2023 training / 2024–2025 test vs. 2025 training / 2026 H1 test). Both nonetheless fail in the same direction and by a similar order of magnitude relative to their respective benchmarks (Section 3.1). Because the two setups were not built to resemble each other, this agreement is reasonably strong evidence for the claim motivating this paper's entire architectural investigation: **single-policy RL using a pretrained embedding-based state and a dense action space is not a reliable starting point for continuous portfolio allocation** (the stronger original claim, "independent of asset class," is not supported by the third market described below).

**A third market complicates the claim.** We checked the same question (does single-policy PPO beat equal-weight?) on 44 U.S. large-cap stocks (11 GICS sectors, 2015–2025, `us_equity_hrl_generalization_colab.ipynb`). Rather than building a new embedding backbone — itself a months-scale undertaking — this check reuses the raw price-window state representation already validated by the Section 6.6 EIIE baseline (no embeddings). That is, this check varies the state representation along with the market. Result: single-policy PPO finished the 2024–2025 test window essentially tied with equal-weight (+46.20%/Sharpe 1.498 vs. +45.74%/Sharpe 1.473) — the dramatic failure seen on Korean equities and crypto does not reproduce. As already noted in Section 1.1, this is the second time this pattern appears in this project's own data: "failure reproduced" (Korean equities, the original crypto agent) is embedding-based in both cases, and "failure not reproduced" (crypto EIIE, U.S. equities) is raw-price-window-based in both cases.

**A follow-up that directly controls the confound.** Because market and architecture were fully confounded above, we ran a further check on the same U.S. large-cap market, the same 44 stocks, and the same 2024–2025 test window, this time equipping the state with Chronos zero-shot embeddings (`us_equity_embedding_confound_colab.ipynb`, skipping the MTGNN training stage and using only the pretrained Chronos encoder at inference time — one of the two components of the original hybrid backbone from Section 3.2) — i.e., the same family of state representation used for Korean equities and crypto. This holds the market fixed and varies only the architecture. Result: performance moved from "tied" to "clearly behind" equal-weight (test Sharpe 1.498 → 1.252, against equal-weight's 1.477), accompanied by a 24x jump in average turnover (0.38% to 9.2–9.4% per step) — the same mechanism documented for v1 in Section 4.1 ("turnover erodes alpha"). **Architecture is a genuine causal factor** in the failure — though not as catastrophic as on crypto (Sharpe −4.209) or Korean equities (0.100 vs. 1.043). We read this as architecture explaining the *direction* of the failure (why it occurs — turnover instability induced by an embedding-based dense action space) and market or regime modulating its *severity* — not a either/or explanation, but both factors operating together. We flag one caveat on the strength of this causal claim: unlike the deployed policy (validated across 16 seeds, Section 6.1), this confound-controlling comparison is a single training run in each arm, with no seed-robustness check of its own. The two pieces of evidence differ in how exposed they are to this gap: the 24x turnover jump is a large, qualitative shift, mechanistically consistent with the cost-erosion pattern documented for v1 (Section 4.1), and not plausibly an artifact of run-to-run noise at this scale; the smaller Sharpe change (1.498→1.252) is harder to rule out as noise on its own, given that seed-to-seed test Sharpe variation of comparable magnitude has been observed elsewhere in this paper (Section 6.1: $\sigma=0.896$ across five additional seeds of crypto's $\beta=-30$ specialist, albeit for a different architecture and market, so not a direct bound on noise here). The controlled design (market, universe, test window, environment, and reward held fixed, with only the state representation varied) supports a causal reading in principle, but we cannot yet rule out that a portion of the observed Sharpe gap specifically is run-to-run noise rather than an architecture effect; repeating both arms across multiple seeds is the natural next step to firm up this claim (Section 8, Limitation 1).

**What one market does not support.** The core structural interventions in the rest of this paper — the structural fix via sparse action spaces (Section 4.4) and the router's collapse into a constant function (Section 4.5) — remain crypto-only: their motivation is specific to the 44-asset dense action space, and, as described below, we do not port them to the 5-dimensional KOSPI action space. The Q-teacher-shaped diversity gap (Section 4.3) and the validation/test consistency diagnostic (Section 4.6) are partially tested by the reduced-scope KOSPI experiment described below. The KOSPI track originally concluded, after its single-policy failure, with a simpler daily-rebalancing PPO baseline (documented in the project's supplementary technical log, outside this paper's scope) rather than an equivalent hierarchical redesign.

Before reporting this reduced-scope test, one framing issue needs to be resolved first: EarnHFT's core motivation (Section 2.1) is the transaction-cost drag that compounds under second-level rebalancing, and Sections 4.1–4.2's crypto v1 experiment reproduces and fixes exactly that problem. KOSPI, however, rebalances daily — a cadence at which the problem EarnHFT was designed to solve has essentially no room to arise, since a once-a-day policy cannot accumulate the kind of compounding cost drag documented in Section 4.1. The KOSPI test below should therefore not be read as asking whether EarnHFT's core high-frequency mechanism also works in a slower market; read that way, the comparison is timescale-mismatched from the start. What it can legitimately test is a narrower, separate hypothesis this paper's own evidence already supports: the confound-controlling experiment above (and Limitation 1, Section 8) identifies a second, distinct causal pathway — turnover instability driven by the pretrained embedding-based state representation itself, rather than by transaction costs — which in principle can arise regardless of rebalancing cadence. KOSPI's own single-policy PPO failure (Section 1.1, Sharpe 0.100 vs. 1.043) is, on timescale grounds alone, more consistent with this architecture-driven pathway than with crypto's cost-compounding one. Read this way, the test below asks whether the same remedy that fixed architecture-driven instability on crypto — specialist diversity plus inference-time smoothing — also transfers to the same instability at a different cadence, not whether EarnHFT's high-frequency-specific mechanism itself generalizes to slow markets.

**KOSPI: partially closing this gap with the final recipe.** Reimplementing the full Section 4 cascade — including the top-$K$-plus-gate structural fix and the router experiments — on KOSPI remains a substantial undertaking, comparable in scope to repeating most of Section 4 on a second dataset, and we still do not attempt it. Instead we run a smaller test: porting only the already-validated final recipe — a Q-teacher-free $\beta$-conditioned specialist pool (Stage A/B, priority sampling, no router) plus inference-time exponential smoothing — to KOSPI's 5-cluster portfolio environment. We omit the top-$K$-plus-gate mechanism (Section 4.4), which is a structural response to dense-softmax collapse specific to the 44-asset setting, and the router, which was ultimately confirmed to contribute zero value in crypto (Sections 4.5–4.6). We exclude 2023 entirely from training to obtain a genuinely unseen validation window, and hold the 2024–2025 test period identical to the original single-policy baseline reported in Section 1.1 for direct comparability.

The result reproduces only partially and is, on balance, negative. The diversity diagnostic differs from v3's outright collapse in Section 4.3: of the four specialists, $\beta=30$ is clearly distinguished from the others (weight-trajectory correlation 0.22–0.51), but the extreme $\beta$ pair ($\beta=\pm90$) converges to essentially the same policy (correlation 0.955) — neither full collapse nor full diversity. The direction of inertial smoothing also differs: only $\beta=-30$ favors low $\alpha$ (strong inertia) in validation as in crypto, while the other three specialists favor high $\alpha$ (weak inertia) instead — plausibly because KOSPI's daily rebalancing cadence, unlike crypto's 30-minute cycle, has little high-frequency noise for inertia to absorb in the first place. Critically, the candidate selected purely on validation Sharpe ($\beta=90$, $\alpha=0.3$) beats equal-weight in validation (Sharpe 1.477 vs. 1.306) but fails outright in test (Sharpe 0.137 vs. 1.043, comparable to the original single-policy PPO's 0.100) — applying the same validation/test consistency criterion from Section 4.6 to KOSPI rejects this candidate, in the same form as (though the opposite direction of) the crypto $\beta=30$ case: strong in one period, weak in the other.

We read this as a result with two implications. First, it shows the validation/test consistency diagnostic working as intended on a new market — the same procedure developed on crypto caught, on KOSPI, a candidate that headline validation-period numbers alone would have gotten deployed. Second, crypto's specific mechanisms (the exact shape of the diversity collapse, the benefit of inertial smoothing) do not transfer as-is — both equal-weight and the original single-policy PPO beat this KOSPI HRL candidate, meaning that, unlike on crypto, no RL-family strategy we have tested yet beats equal-weight on KOSPI. Because this test omits Section 4.4's structural intervention, it does not let us conclude that the full Section 4 cascade fails to generalize to KOSPI — only that (a) the degree of Q-teacher-free diversity collapse may depend on asset count and action-space dimensionality, and (b) the validation/test consistency diagnostic itself appears to be a market-agnostic, robust methodological tool. Porting the full structural intervention of Section 4.4 to KOSPI remains future work. Read against the narrower framing established above, this negative result means the remedy for *architecture-driven* turnover instability does not transfer to KOSPI either — it is not evidence that EarnHFT's high-frequency-specific mechanism fails in slow markets, a claim this experiment was never positioned to test in the first place.

**U.S. large-cap equities: the same final recipe, a third result.** On top of the same raw price-window environment used for the motivating single-policy check above, we port the same reduced recipe used for KOSPI (Q-teacher-free $\beta$-conditioned specialist pool plus inference-time smoothing, no top-$K$-plus-gate, no router) — reused unchanged specifically so the KOSPI and U.S. equity secondary-market checks remain directly comparable to each other, not because the top-$K$-plus-gate mechanism is structurally inapplicable here the way it is to KOSPI's 5-dimensional action space (U.S. equities shares crypto's 44-asset action space, so that specific argument does not apply); porting the full v4 structural fix to U.S. equities specifically remains untested (Section 8, Limitation 1). The diversity diagnostic shows average pairwise weight-trajectory correlation of 0.86–0.98 across the four specialists (top-10 holdings Jaccard overlap 0.54–0.73) — much closer to v3's outright collapse (Section 4.3) than to KOSPI's partial diversity. Inertial smoothing shows yet another pattern: **all four** $\beta$ values favor $\alpha=1.0$ (no smoothing at all) in validation — unlike crypto ("inertia always wins") or KOSPI (mixed), plausibly because a daily rebalancing cadence has little of the high-frequency cost that inertia absorbs on crypto's 30-minute cycle (a hypothesis, untested). The candidate selected on validation Sharpe ($\beta=-90$, $\alpha=1.0$) edges out equal-weight in validation (Sharpe 1.553 vs. 1.525) but is **statistically indistinguishable** from equal-weight (1.473) and single-policy PPO (1.498) in test — 1.479, even marginally below the single policy. Critically, the validation/test Sharpe gap itself is small (0.074) — by that diagnostic alone, this candidate reads as "consistent." But it is consistent at being *no better than* equal-weight, not consistent at *beating* it.

This last point is the mirror image of the KOSPI case: the KOSPI HRL candidate was rejected by a large validation/test gap, while the U.S. equity HRL candidate passes the gap criterion but delivers a result with nothing to be consistent *about*. Both failures show that Section 4.6's success criterion is a conjunction, not a single number — **both** a small validation/test gap **and** positive performance are required. The risk-parity baseline in Section 6.5 (gap 0.003, the smallest in this paper, but negative in both periods) makes the identical point independently: a small gap alone does not certify a policy's quality.

**Synthesizing three markets.**

| Market | State representation | Single policy vs. equal-weight (§1.1) | Final HRL recipe vs. equal-weight | Val/test gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Crypto (deployed) | Embeddings | Fails badly (Sharpe −4.209 vs. 0.367) | **Dominates (+5.66% vs. +2.66%, 3x Sharpe)** | Small (0.158) |
| KOSPI | Embeddings | Fails badly (Sharpe 0.100 vs. 1.043) | Wins in validation, fails outright in test | **Large (1.340)** |
| U.S. large-cap | Raw price window | No failure (essentially tied, 1.498 vs. 1.473) | Indistinguishable from equal-weight, both periods | Small (0.074, but moot) |
| U.S. large-cap (confound-controlled) | **Embeddings** (market held fixed) | Clearly behind (Sharpe 1.252 vs. 1.477) | (not tested) | — |

The fourth row is the crux of this table: holding the market fixed at U.S. large-cap and switching only the state representation, from raw price windows to embeddings, moved performance from "tied" to "clearly behind" — directly confirming, within this project's own data, that architecture is a cause of the failure. But the size of that drop (1.498 → 1.252) falls well short of the collapse seen on crypto (−4.209) or KOSPI (0.100 vs. 1.043), which also implicates market or regime as a separate modulator of severity. The three markets otherwise exhibit three distinct outcomes: crypto is the only success this paper tests (small gap plus genuine outperformance); KOSPI is a rejection caught by the gap diagnostic; U.S. large-cap is a small gap with nothing behind it. In two of the three markets, the final recipe fails to beat equal-weight — this paper has no basis for claiming its deployed policy generalizes across markets, and does not make that claim. What we do report is that the same diagnostic procedure established on crypto (validation/test consistency), applied unchanged to two different markets, produced defensible — if negative — verdicts in both cases, and that the single-policy failure motivating Section 1.1 is caused by architecture and modulated in severity by market or regime. That is the full extent of what this paper can claim about the portability of its methodology and failure mechanisms, as distinct from the portability of the deployed policy itself.

**Same asset class, different timescale: a point-in-time 13-asset multi-year walk-forward.** The three-market comparison above asked how far the final recipe transfers when the *market* changes. Limitation 5 (Section 8) wanted to ask the same question within crypto itself, along the *time* axis, but concluded that genuine multi-year walk-forward was structurally impossible on the 44-asset universe because of listing age. This subsection reports a follow-up that partially overturns that conclusion.

*Constructing a point-in-time universe.* Rather than re-selecting the universe by today's trading volume, we consulted CoinMarketCap's historical snapshot for 2021-01-01 (top-20 by market capitalization) and, after excluding stablecoins (USDT, USDC) and a wrapped asset (WBTC), arrived at 17 "real" assets. Of these, 13 (BTC, ETH, XRP, LTC, BCH, ADA, BNB, LINK, XLM, TRX, DOT, THETA, XTZ) were confirmed still active on Binance via the exchange's API at verification time; 4 (BSV, EOS, XMR, XEM) were confirmed inactive or delisted — these four are the residual survivorship bias this experiment cannot resolve (point-in-time selection removes look-ahead bias, but the structural limitation that delisted-coin history is unavailable from free sources remains). Ranks 21–50 could not be verified due to scraping limitations, so this universe is strictly scoped to "top-20 by January 2021 market cap that is still tradable today" — it does not claim the same breadth as the original 44-asset track.

*Split and method.* Train 2021–2023 (3 years) / validation 2024 (1 year, genuinely unseen) / test 2025–2026 (through the end of available data, roughly 1.5 years) — the same split structure as the KOSPI track. We port the already-validated final recipe unchanged: a Q-teacher-free $\beta$-conditioned specialist pool (Stage A/B, no router) plus inference-time smoothing, omitting top-$K$-plus-gate since its structural motivation is weak at this small a scale.

*First result — an extreme regime reversal.* The validation period (2024) was an extraordinary bull run in which equal-weight alone returned +98.9% (Sharpe 1.371). Sweeping all 28 $\beta \times \alpha$ combinations on this period selected $\beta=-30$, $\alpha=0.01$ (validation Sharpe 1.434, narrowly ahead of equal-weight), which we then ran once on the test period (2025–2026, in which equal-weight fell −55.7%). The result: net return −46.12% (Sharpe −0.297) — better than equal-weight (−55.72%, Sharpe −0.393) and with a smaller drawdown (−62.46% vs. −65.69%, though the margin is only 3.2 points, far short of the 44-asset experiment's 82% relative reduction) — but a validation-to-test Sharpe gap of 1.731, approaching this paper's largest observed gap anywhere (momentum's 2.742) and far beyond the deployed policy's 0.158. Running all four $\beta$ candidates standalone shows the same pattern without exception (gaps of 1.662–1.805).

*Second check — confirming the reversal is not a fluke of one split.* To test whether this failure is an artifact of the specific "2024 bull into 2025 crash" pairing, we reused the same four frozen $\beta$ policies (trained once on 2021–2023, no retraining) and repeated the identical procedure (select on validation, evaluate once on test) across four adjacent six-month blocks spanning 2024–2026. All four folds exceeded a 0.3 gap (0.773 / 2.503 / 0.330 / 2.305, mean 1.478) — even the one fold with the smallest gap (2025 H1 → H2, 0.330) had validation and test Sharpes of only 0.03 and 0.36, offering little to be consistent about in the first place. None of five independent (validation, test) observations were genuinely robust.

*Third check — ruling out diversity collapse as the cause.* Given the v3 precedent (Section 4.3), we first had to rule out that this failure was, once again, "diversity never emerged." Pairwise weight-trajectory correlation among the four $\beta$ experts over the test period averaged −0.153 (range −0.357 to +0.287) — far better separated than v3's near-total collapse ($\approx$1), and even better separated than the structurally-fixed v4 (0.109–0.545) or the KOSPI reduced recipe (0.22–0.51); some pairs are even negatively correlated. Top holdings differ meaningfully by $\beta$ ($\beta=-90$ favors ADA/DOT/ETH; $\beta=-30$ favors BCH/BNB/BTC; and so on). **Diversity was genuine here, and every candidate still failed** — a more fundamental failure than v3's, since it rules out the easier fix ("restore diversity") that worked for the 44-asset track.

*Interpretation.* The validation/test consistency diagnostic itself again worked exactly as designed — across all five independent observations, it correctly flagged every candidate as unreliable, which strengthens rather than weakens Section 6's methodological contribution. But this result is not direct evidence that the 44-asset, 18-month deployed policy collapses at the same scale (it is a different model, trained on a different universe and different data) — what it does mean is that this paper's own evidence, now that the time axis has actually been extended, argues against treating "a small validation/test gap" as proof of genuine multi-year robustness. This is why the 44-asset experiment's downside-protection profile (Section 1.4, Contribution 4) should not be read as evidence of robustness validated over a long horizon.

## 8. Limitations

We list the limitations of this study directly, in the order they arise across Sections 3–7, rather than only in aggregate:

1. **The core structural interventions of the detailed cascade (Section 4) remain validated on one market, and the final recipe beat equal-weight on only one of three markets tested.** As discussed in Section 7, the structural fix (Section 4.4) and router collapse (Section 4.5) are interventions specific to the 44-asset dense action space, not ported to KOSPI or U.S. equities, and remain crypto-specific findings. A related framing caveat, introduced in Section 3.1 and addressed directly in Section 7: both tracks in this paper run several orders of magnitude slower than EarnHFT's original second-level setting, and KOSPI's daily cadence in particular lacks the high-frequency transaction-cost mechanism EarnHFT's hierarchical structure was designed to address in the first place — so the KOSPI results below should be read as testing whether a remedy for a *different*, frequency-agnostic failure mode (architecture-driven turnover instability, discussed later in this same item) transfers across markets, not as a literal generalization test of EarnHFT's high-frequency mechanism itself. This caveat does not apply in the same way to the crypto track, whose 30-minute rebalancing cadence — while still far slower than EarnHFT's original setting — does retain a real, demonstrated transaction-cost-drag mechanism (Section 4.1). The reason differs across the two markets: KOSPI's 5-dimensional action space makes the top-$K$-plus-gate mechanism structurally inapplicable, but U.S. equities shares crypto's 44-asset action space, so that argument does not extend there — the reduced recipe was used instead to keep the two secondary-market checks directly comparable to each other, and porting the full v4 structural fix to U.S. equities specifically has simply not been done, left for future work. The two reduced-scope final-recipe experiments reported in Section 7 (KOSPI, U.S. large-cap) provide solid evidence for the validation/test consistency diagnostic itself (it reached a defensible verdict in both cases), but the deployed recipe failed to beat equal-weight in both markets — by a large validation/test gap on KOSPI, and by simply having no edge to be consistent about on U.S. equities despite a small gap there. The claim that crypto's specific mechanisms (the exact shape of diversity collapse, the benefit of inertial smoothing, the final performance) generalize across markets is therefore not supported by this data — only the diagnostic procedure's portability is. The initial U.S. equity check also varied the state representation along with the market (Section 1.1, Section 7 — raw price windows instead of embeddings, a deliberate choice made for tractability), so a follow-up held the market fixed and equipped the same U.S. equity data with Chronos zero-shot embeddings instead (Section 7). Performance dropped from tied-with-equal-weight to clearly behind it, consistent with architecture as a genuine causal factor — but the drop was far smaller than the collapse observed on crypto or KOSPI, indicating market or regime independently modulates the severity of the failure. This resolves the confound only partially: we now have evidence that architecture matters, not just that it is correlated with market in this dataset, but we cannot yet quantify how much of the *crypto- and KOSPI-scale* collapse is attributable to architecture versus regime-specific factors (e.g., asset correlation structure, trend strength) that a single follow-up cannot isolate. The confound-controlling comparison itself is also a single training run per arm, unlike the 16-seed validation behind the deployed policy (Section 6.1) — the controlled design (only the state representation varied, everything else held fixed) supports a causal reading, but run-to-run noise has not been ruled out as a partial explanation for the observed gap. A fully resolved account would require repeating this embedding-vs-raw-window comparison across multiple seeds and on additional markets or regimes, left for future work. Separately, the $\lambda$-sweep in Section 4.3 — a controlled experiment manipulating objective-level signal strength as a single variable and observing a monotonic response in the predicted direction — supports the reading that v3's diversity collapse is controllable via the diagnosed mechanism rather than an artifact of incidental implementation-specific tuning, but this too is confirmed only within the v3 architecture family; empirically repeating this argument on hierarchical RL families other than EarnHFT (e.g., Option-Critic) remains undone.
2. **PPO/DQN hyperparameters remain fixed without a sweep, and the post-hoc top-K sweep is only partial.** The PPO/DQN hyperparameters in Section 3.3 were chosen once, empirically, and held fixed across all of v1–v4 specifically to keep the cross-configuration comparisons in Section 5 uncontaminated by unequal tuning effort — but this means we cannot rule out that different PPO settings would materially change any single configuration's result. $K=10$ in the top-$K$ selection (Section 4.4) was later swept post hoc over neighboring values $\{5,7,15\}$ (Section 6.8), confirming that $K=10$ is the only swept value to pass the validation/test consistency criterion — though the actual swept range ($\{5,7,10,15\}$) does not exactly match the range this paragraph originally flagged as a future candidate ($\{5,10,15,20\}$), and wider values (notably $K=20$) remain untested. The same sweep also revealed that the confidence logits used for top-$K$ selection carry near-zero rank correlation (Spearman IC) with realized returns (Section 6.8) — differences in performance across $K$ should not be over-interpreted as evidence of accurate stock selection.
3. **DQN router training is not deterministic even at fixed seed, and this paper substitutes standard DQN for the original paper's DDQN.** Repeated training runs of the router (Section 4.1, 4.5) with identical seeds and hyperparameters have been observed to vary by roughly ±8 percentage points in net return, attributed to GPU-level non-determinism in the training pipeline. This means single-run comparisons involving a freshly trained router — including the original-vs-matched router comparison in Section 4.5, prior to the decisive ablation that showed both had collapsed to the same constant policy — carry irreducible noise on top of whatever architectural difference is being measured, and any future router-based configuration should be evaluated over repeated training runs before its result is trusted in isolation. Separately, the original EarnHFT paper uses DDQN for both Stage I and Stage III (Section 2.4), while this paper uses standard DQN for implementation simplicity — DDQN was designed specifically to correct standard DQN's overestimation bias, so the early convergence to a single fixed action observed in the router (Section 4.5) cannot be fully attributed to the diversity-signal-absence diagnosis proposed here rather than, partly or wholly, to standard DQN's known overestimation bias. We tested this directly: retraining the matched router with everything else held fixed and only DQN swapped for DDQN, the router still converged 100% to $\beta=30$ on both splits (92/92 chunks on validation, 195/195 on test), and net return and Sharpe ratio matched the original DQN-based matched router to two decimal places (validation $-0.19\%$/Sharpe $0.025$, test $+7.48\%$/Sharpe $3.046$). Reproducing the identical collapse under an algorithm specifically designed to correct overestimation bias suggests that standard DQN's overestimation bias was not the cause — the observed router collapse appears attributable, independent of algorithm choice, to the mechanism diagnosed in Section 4.5 (matching the episode\_progress training/serving distributions removed the router's diversity signal entirely).
4. **The front-loaded-returns question in Section 4.4 is not fully resolved.** The v4 policy pool's test-period return is front-loaded (+5.47% in the first half of the window vs. +2.64% in the second), and while turnover and asset-rotation diagnostics rule out a pure static-position artifact, a full explanation of the front/back-half asymmetry (e.g., a regime-decomposition analysis) has not been carried out.
5. **Multi-year walk-forward validation was not possible on the original 44-asset universe — but we ran it on a point-in-time reduced universe, and it failed.** Only 23 of the 44 assets have price history predating 2022, and none predate 2021, so a genuine multi-year walk-forward evaluation (as opposed to the single-year validation/test split used throughout Sections 4–6) is not achievable on the full universe without first shrinking it to a survivorship-biased subset. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Section 6.2) is used as a partial substitute, but it corrects for a different bias (Section 6.2) than a multi-year walk-forward would, and does not fully close this gap. As reported in Section 7, we constructed a point-in-time 13-asset universe by cross-referencing CoinMarketCap's January 2021 market-cap snapshot against live exchange listings, and ran a genuine multi-year walk-forward (train 2021–2023, validate 2024, test 2025–2026) with the same methodology behind the deployed policy ($\beta$-conditioned specialist pool plus validation-selected smoothing). The result: across five independent (validation, test) observations — one formal split plus four no-retraining rolling-refit folds — the validation/test Sharpe gap exceeded the 0.2 threshold in every single case (0.33–2.50, mean 1.48). A separate diagnostic ruled out diversity collapse (Section 4.3's cause for v3) as the explanation — the four $\beta$ experts' weight-trajectory correlations averaged −0.153, better separated than even v4 or KOSPI. This is not direct evidence that the 44-asset, 18-month deployed policy collapses at the same scale (it is a different model, on a different universe, trained on different data) — but it means this paper's central diagnostic criterion, "a small validation/test gap," is not supported by this data as a guarantee of genuine multi-year robustness.
6. **No configuration in this study reaches conventional statistical significance under DSR.** Both the deployed seed and the raw-maximum seed have DSR below the conventional 0.95 threshold at both $N=6$ and $N=16$ (Section 6.2). The deployment argument in this paper rests on validation/test consistency (Section 4.6, 6.1), not on achieving formal statistical significance — readers should not conflate "the most robust configuration we tested" with "a result proven significant by a multiple-comparisons-corrected test."
7. **The transaction-cost model is a fixed proportional fee with no slippage or market-impact term.** $\tau=0.001$ matches Binance's standard taker fee at the account's current scale, but does not model slippage or the price impact of larger orders; this is adequate at the live account's current size (Section 9) but would need revisiting before drawing conclusions at meaningfully larger capital. A post-hoc sensitivity sweep over $\tau \in \{0.05\%, \ldots, 0.2\%\}$ (0.5–2x) shows this assumption is not load-bearing for the conclusion — even at 2x cost, the deployed policy's test Sharpe (0.955) remains 2.6x the equal-weight benchmark (0.367) — though this sweep still leaves the fixed-proportional-fee model itself, and the slippage/market-impact gap, unaddressed.
8. **The EIIE and LSRE-CAAN baselines (Section 6.6) reimplement only the network topology, not the original training procedure.** Both original papers' training methods (EIIE's OSBL, LSRE-CAAN's direct policy gradient) are replaced with this paper's PPO pipeline; the comparisons in Section 6.6 should be read as "each architecture under our training regime," not as full reproductions of Jiang, Xu, & Liang (2017) or Li, Zhang, Yang, & Chen (2023).
9. **This is an empirical case study and diagnostic methodology, not a new algorithm.** Every architectural component in Sections 4 and the diagnostics in Section 6 are adaptations of existing published methods (Section 2); the paper's contribution is the documented failure cascade and the validation methodology built to interrogate it, which is why we position this work for an applied-science venue (Section 1) rather than a methods-focused main research track.
10. **Live deployment evidence is preliminary.** The final policy (Section 4.6) is running on a real exchange account as of this writing, but the live trading log has been accumulating only since 2026-07-29 and is not yet long enough to serve as an independent validation of the backtested results reported in Sections 5–6; we report the live deployment as the current operational status of this work, not as additional evidence for its claims.

## 9. Conclusion

This paper traced what happens when a hierarchical reinforcement learning architecture designed for single-asset, discrete-position high-frequency trading (EarnHFT) is adapted to continuous, multi-asset portfolio allocation, across independently built market datasets. The starting motivation — that single-policy RL using a pretrained, embedding-based state fails to beat equal-weighting — held up on two markets (Korean equities, cryptocurrency), but did not reproduce on a third (U.S. large-cap equities) initially checked with a raw price-window state representation instead. A follow-up that held the market fixed and re-equipped the same U.S. equity data with an embedding-based state (Section 7) reversed this, moving performance from "tied" back to "clearly behind" — consistent with architecture as a genuine causal factor in the failure, though not as catastrophic as on crypto or Korean equities, implicating market or regime as a separate modulator of severity (Section 7, Limitation 1). The subsequent architectural investigation, conducted on the cryptocurrency track, surfaced a sequence of failures that would not have been visible from headline test-period metrics alone: a cost-driven signal loss fixable only at inference time, not in training; a diversity collapse traceable to the absence of a dynamic-programming teacher signal; a structural fix that restored diversity but left the resulting policy's routing layer to quietly degenerate into a constant function; and a specific specialist agent whose apparent test-period superiority turned out, on a targeted validation/test comparison, to be overfitting rather than skill. The policy that survived this process — a single specialist agent combined with a fixed-rate inference-time smoothing filter, with no router at all — is also the simplest configuration considered at any point in the study, and it is the only one of ten strategies tested (spanning classical, deep-learning, and RL baselines) to achieve consistently positive performance across both a held-out validation period and an out-of-sample test period, despite not achieving the highest raw return of any strategy tested.

We take two lessons from this arc to be of use beyond this specific project. First, when adapting a method across a significant change in problem structure — here, from single-asset discrete actions to multi-asset continuous ones — it is worth explicitly identifying which components of the original method are load-bearing for its key mechanism (diversity, in EarnHFT's case) before assuming a partial port will preserve that mechanism; Section 4.3 and its corroborating single-asset check (Section 6.7) show this can be diagnosed directly rather than discovered by accident. Second, and more broadly, a policy's headline test-period performance is not sufficient evidence of its quality on its own — the validation/test Sharpe gap used throughout Sections 4.6–6 caught a router collapse (Section 4.5) and an overfit specialist (Section 4.6) that Sharpe ratio and maximum drawdown, evaluated on the test period alone, did not. We report this diagnostic, and its relationship to the (distinct, complementary) Deflated Sharpe Ratio, as a methodological contribution independent of the specific trading results.

The policy described in Section 4.6 is currently deployed on a live exchange account. The transaction-cost sensitivity sweep (Section 8, limitation 7), two reduced-scope generalization tests on KOSPI and U.S. large-cap equities (Section 7), and a follow-up that directly controlled the market/architecture confound (Section 7 — confirming architecture as a causal factor, with market/regime left as a likely modulator of severity) are now complete — the final recipe beat equal-weight on only one of the three markets tested, but the validation/test consistency diagnostic reached a defensible verdict on the new markets, and the causal structure behind Section 1.1's motivating failure is now better understood. Extending the evidence base with a longer live-trading record, reimplementing the full Section 4 cascade — including the structural intervention of Section 4.4 — on the KOSPI universe, and repeating the embedding-vs-raw-window comparison across additional markets or regimes to quantify architecture's contribution relative to market/regime, are the most direct remaining next steps, and are left for future work given this paper's present scope and timeline. A separate line of work — proposing and validating a new algorithmic mechanism for restoring specialist diversity without a dynamic-programming teacher, rather than documenting how its absence manifests — would be needed to meet the novelty bar of a methods-focused main research track; we regard the present paper, targeted at an applied-science venue, as a foundation for that follow-on work rather than a substitute for it.
